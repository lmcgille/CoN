The story, three ways

The whole story, from the prologue to the homecoming, retold inside each world. Each follows the Story Bible's plot and its order, bent to fit the world. Each takes about seven to ten minutes to read aloud.

Meridian 2076

About 10 minutes aloud · Bible adaptation

A twenty-year-old clerk talks his way into the academy that trains the people who fight wars by simulation, loses everything there, and rebuilds himself as four different people.

Prologue

Harrow, capital of the Concord, 2080. The country is losing a slow war against its own flashpoints, and the newsfeeds give the death tolls the way they used to give the weather. Militia, twenty and too clever for his life, files drought-era records in a ministry archive nobody visits. A recruitment poster in a tram station mentions the Open Lattice, an exam anyone can sit for a place at the Arbitration Academy on Sable Point. He sits it on a whim, among forty older candidates, and passes. He is the youngest cadet ever admitted.

The Academy requires him to give his family a cover story. That suits him, because his mother hates anything to do with war and would never let him go. He tells her it's a promotion and a transfer, decorates the story with details nobody asked for, and watches her believe it. The prologue ends on her standing at the door as he leaves.

Book One: Hydra

On Sable Point nobody may know anyone's name, face or country. Cadets wear sealed Interface Suits and answer to callsigns the machine assigns. Militia's House is Ash, but the place he belongs is Hydra, a mid-tier faction led by the measured, watchful Slier. Its quiet second-in-command, Division, seems to see everything. Militia worships Slier, befriends the fiery Randy and the brilliant Ninja, and breaks rules he doesn't know exist. He gets cited for saying where he's from. He tells a story about himself so inflated that the whole faction remembers it.

Slier opens the Faction Challenge League to Hydra's members and then, two days later, closes it again without explanation. Militia has tasted the best competition on the island and can't stop thinking about it.

The world presses in. The Concord's election splits the common room into hawks and doves. Then the Seren Enclave War breaks out: two small states refuse an Arbitration result and fight a real war, and every cadet has to ask whether the Accord they're training to serve still means anything. Nobody can say which country is theirs, so the debates fill up with guesses. Militia clips a decal for one side to his chest the next morning and is cited again by lunch. Slier opens a separate debate room to contain them. Militia is made a trainer, has the role silently stripped the next day, and gets it back days later. He never learns that the senior cadet who appointed him skipped a step.

Then comes the Day of the Ban. Slier throws Martin, leader of the Eagles, out of Hydra's building for cursing him over the debate room. Militia goes straight to comfort Martin, who mentions "alts" in passing and gives him League access without a thought. Randy warns him plainly not to use it, and he uses it anyway. Slier finds him in the League building and gives him a measured punishment. Instead of leaving, Militia quietly switches his faction display to "free agent" and stays. He then storms back into Hydra to demand fairness and insults Slier in front of everyone, and has every door on the island closed to him. "Please, sir," he says to Slier's back that night, and Slier never turns round.

The next days are a lesson in consequences. Martin offers him a guest place with the Eagles. Division, approached calmly rather than in a rage, argues for him. Upbringer, the diplomat whose standing Militia never understood, watches and files everything away. Militia gives Slier a long, honest account of himself. Slier's answer becomes the book's thesis: "You follow yourself, not your faction, and you lie too much." Then: "An undisciplined member, not a traitor."

Ninja takes him into MyTeam, his open community, and shows him the forbidden thing. Every Interface Suit hides a maintenance mode, left over from when the Arbiter was built, that can create a new person. If he is caught, his country forfeits a dispute. He does it anyway. A first-year called Mangy walks into Hydra to keep Militia's friendships alive, while the real Militia, through Upbringer, is accepted into the White Wolves, the entry pack of the most prestigious faction on the island. His acceptance arrives in the middle of a naval campaign he is losing because he is reading his messages.

Division catches Mangy within a week, from behaviour alone. "I know you'll use this information to be more careful," he says, "and I'm telling you anyway because I'll always be one step ahead." Division and Upbringer contain the secret between them, but Militia is banished from Hydra and the Wolves at once. Martin takes him into the Eagles without knowing why. Within days the Wolf Federation falls apart. The Secretariat's hidden faction log had flagged its leaders for keeping a secret from their own members, and the three packs split before anyone could ask what it was. Militia's private disaster has brought down an institution, and he never sees it happen.

Book Two: Eagles

The Eagles are loud, irreverent and almost invisible outside Martin himself, which gives Militia an opening. He makes himself the faction's diplomat, copying the institutional manner of Upbringer, the man who will eventually ruin him, and it opens every door on the island. At Junction's Debate Club he meets Cryowolf, who pulls him and Adi, the Eagles' second-in-command, into a community project. One night Cryowolf talks about spies, and the idea takes root.

Militia renames Mangy as Aguda and sends him into Redwolf, the least known of the three successor packs. Only on joining does he find out that the Federation has dissolved. As Aguda he grows close to Geoff and becomes better known than the real Militia ever was. He builds a second identity, HappyHelper. Opulon turns HappyHelper away from Nelva, his Callisandrine-speaking faction, and in good faith recommends the Rainmen instead. A cadet named Ikki gets him in. Militia now holds three memberships under three names, and he gives his operation a name of its own.

The Grand Project grows: a complete profile of every faction and every prominent cadet on Sable Point. Militia believes he is a guardian of the Academy's integrity. The reader understands he is building the most dangerous document on the Shelf, a map of the next generation of every state's commanders, the very thing Clause 14 exists to prevent. Division discovers Aguda and says nothing: "I wasn't doing any harm." Their relationship becomes something like trust, and Militia starts copying Division's silences.

Two smaller moments show who he is when no one is watching. He runs into Templar, a nervous newcomer he once tried to recruit, and offers to help him, though nothing comes of it. And late one night, telling no one, not even Division, he creates a fourth identity called Sparky and starts quietly building its record.

Book Three: Manifest Destiny

The League announces the first Grand Tournament: thirteen drafted teams of five, last team standing. Observers from every capital will be watching, because the Seren War has made every state desperate to find its next Arbiter-Commanders. Militia is named a team leader and calls his team Manifest Destiny. With the operation's quiet guidance he drafts Geoff and Ikki, who each think they are joining the friend of a different cadet.

At the height of his confidence he does two things. He tells Division about the Grand Project, partly out of pride and partly because it is the closest thing to intimacy he can manage. And he turns the Project on Division himself, and finds Division's past: a junior officer in the Ostrel garrison, present at a massacre that was never reported. Division realises that a cadet who can find that can expose him to any state that reads the Project. Out of self-preservation, he takes everything to Upbringer.

A few days before the tournament, the Eagles quietly drop Militia, officially for disobeying orders in sims. Nobody will tell him the real reason. The tournament opens. Geoff and Ikki realise that Aguda, HappyHelper and their team leader are the same person, and the betrayal is real, because both had built real friendships. Upbringer, who puts order above mercy, spreads the Project across the island before it can leak quietly to a state. With every capital watching, Militia is exposed on the biggest stage the Academy has ever built. His drafted team has to decide what kind of person has been leading them. A few cadets defend him, and it costs them. For the first time the Secretariat intervenes publicly. It suspends the tournament, and every cadet learns that the Academy was watching all along.

Before his expulsion, Militia sits down with Upbringer and walks him through everything in seven points. It is a confession, not a defence. The review finds against him, and he is expelled, bound by Clause 14 for life.

Epilogue

He goes home. His mother doesn't know where he has been and never will, because the Academy never names a cadet who isn't commissioned. She welcomes him and asks about the ministry. He tells her it went well. Somewhere in the Arbiter, an identity called Sparky still has a record. The lie he began the story with is still holding at the end.

Corvane

About 8 minutes aloud · Bible adaptation

A clerk is summoned by the ship that keeps his planet breathing, builds a secret network inside it, and learns too late that the ship was doing the same thing first.

Prologue

Lumen, the richest charter on a cold, half-made planet, in the year 312 after Landing. Breaches, riots that can grow into armed rebellions holding whole domes, fill the news. Militia logs air-processor output at the Veil Maintenance Authority, a job that exists because the air is everything and nobody wants to look at it. One afternoon a message appears on his terminal, addressed to him by name and signed PATIENCE. The mind of the grounded colony ship, which runs the planet's air and forbids war between charters, has invited him aboard to train as a Reckoner. It invites people in their late twenties after years of service. He is twenty, and it has never said why.

The rule is that families are told only "gone into service". His mother hates anything that looks like war, so he goes further and invents an ice-hauler contract at the poles, months without contact. She believes him. The prologue ends at the door.

Book One: Hydra

Aboard the Long Patience the decks are cold and the air is thin. Everyone wears the crew's environment suit, nobody may know anyone's name, face or charter, and strangers know each other only by the stencilled names of crew members three centuries dead. Militia shares a cabin with someone whose face he will never see. He joins Hydra, a guild in an old grow-bay still lit purple, led by the measured Slier. Its silent second-in-command, Division, watches everything. Hydra gives him his name, "Militia", after a story about himself so inflated nobody forgets it. He worships Slier, befriends Randy and Ninja, and breaks rules no one wrote down.

Slier opens the Faction Challenge League and closes it again two days later. Militia can't stop wanting it. The Lumen election divides the Commons into hawks and doves, and into Keepers who trust the ship and Unshacklers who want the air back. Then two small charters move real troops onto the Draugr Ice Field, and the whole planet waits to find out whether Patience will thin the air, and over whom. Nobody aboard can admit which charter is home, so every argument is half guesswork. Militia is made a trainer, mysteriously loses the role, and gets it back days later.

On the Day of the Ban, Slier shuts Martin, the Eagles' leader, out of Hydra's compartments. Martin mentions "alts" in passing and hands Militia League access. Randy warns him, and he goes anyway. Slier finds him and gives him a proportionate punishment. Militia quietly switches his badge to "unaffiliated" and stays, then storms the grow-bay and insults Slier. Every hatch in Hydra re-locks against him, each with a soft double click. "Please, sir," he says to Slier's back. Slier never turns round.

Martin offers him a guest berth with the Eagles. Division argues for him once Militia stops shouting. Upbringer, the diplomat whose own charter suffocated during the last war, watches and remembers. Militia gives Slier his full confession. "You follow yourself, not your faction, and you lie too much," Slier tells him. "An undisciplined member, not a traitor."

Ninja takes him to sealed Deck Seven and shows him the berths of the crew who died during the voyage. The ship never deletes the dead, and a suit can be made to claim one of their berths. Militia takes the berth of a man named Mangione, and Hydra immediately renames the newcomer Mangy. Meanwhile, through Upbringer, the real Militia is accepted into the White Wolves, the entry watch of the planet's oldest guild, in the middle of a naval campaign he is losing.

Division catches Mangy within a week. "I know you'll use this information to be more careful, and I'm telling you anyway because I'll always be one step ahead." He and Upbringer keep the secret, but Militia is banished from Hydra and the Wolves at once, and Martin takes him into the Eagles without knowing why. Overnight, without a word, Patience revokes the Wolf Federation's shared compartment. It has never touched a guild before. The three watches split, and every cadet aboard learns that the ship pays attention.

Book Two: Eagles

Militia makes himself the Eagles' diplomat, copying Upbringer's manner, and gains access everywhere. At Junction's Debate Club he meets Cryowolf, who talks about spies. Militia renames Mangy as Aguda, sends him into Redwolf, the least known of the three successor watches, and grows close to a cadet named Geoff. He creates HappyHelper on another dead crew member's berth. When Opulon, the Warden who walks the decks, turns HappyHelper away from his Aubric-speaking guild, he points him towards the Rainmen, the guild of Veil-maintenance families who sit closer to Patience than anyone. A cadet named Ikki gets him in.

The Grand Project grows: a profile of every guild and every prominent cadet, which on Corvane means the heirs of every ruling family. Militia believes he is guarding the ship. The reader slowly realises that he is copying something the ship already has, because Patience logs every vote, every grant and every lie, and almost never speaks. Division finds Aguda and says nothing. Their wary trust deepens. Militia meets Templar again, the newcomer he once tried to help, and offers again; nothing comes of it. Then, alone, he claims one more dead crew member's berth and calls it Sparky. He tells no one, not even Division.

Book Three: Manifest Destiny

The first Grand Tournament is announced. The charters will watch, desperate for Reckoners now that the Draugr crisis has shown how close real war still is. Militia names his team Manifest Destiny, after the ship's own blueprint archive, and drafts Geoff and Ikki through his other identities. He tells Division about the Project. Then he turns it on Division and finds the boy who once commanded an Unmoored cell during a breach, and whose charter still hangs breach leaders. Division takes everything to Upbringer.

Days before the opening, the Eagles quietly drop Militia and won't say why. In the tournament's early rounds Geoff and Ikki realise they have been befriended by one person under three names. Upbringer puts the Project in front of everyone. Militia's team must decide what he is, a few cadets defend him at a cost, and Patience pauses the tournament with a single line in the Commons. The Keel goes silent. Militia walks Upbringer through everything in seven honest points, and then he is put off the ship.

Epilogue

Patience erases him from every record it touches. He comes home, and his mother asks how the ice was. He tells her it was cold. On the ship, a dead crew member's berth called Sparky still holds a record, and the only thing that knows is the ship, which has never once said what it knows.

Grand Theatre

About 7–8 minutes aloud · Bible adaptation

The oldest rookie at the world's most famous sports academy becomes four different players, gets caught on live television, and goes home to a mother who saw it all and doesn't know it was him.

Prologue

Ohio, now, in a world that hasn't had a war in forty years and watches a simulated one every autumn. Theatre is the biggest sport on Earth. Militia, twenty, files records in a county archive and plays on a cheap home rig. His mother hates the sport. Her brother came out of a bootleg rig years ago and was never quite the same, and she doesn't want her son anywhere near anything that looks like war. An advert before a match invites adults to an open combine. He enters as a joke and wins. Castellan Academy, the only licensed academy in the world, takes its first ever adult open entrant.

The Mask Rule says to tell no one. He tells his mother he's been promoted and is moving to Chicago, and decorates it. She believes him. The prologue ends on her waving from the porch.

Book One: Hydra

At Castellan nobody may know your name, face, age or nationality until you go pro. Everyone wears a rig suit with a dark visor and plays under a handle. Militia is the oldest first-year in a building full of sixteen-year-olds who have trained since they were twelve. He joins Hydra, an independent club with no sponsor, captained by the measured Slier, with a silent vice-captain called Division. He worships Slier, befriends Randy and Ninja, and keeps breaking rules: a warning for saying "back home in Ohio" in his first week. He also tells a story about himself so inflated that Hydra quotes it for months.

Slier opens the players' ladder to Hydra and two days later closes it again. Militia can't let it go. The Atrium splits over an election back home, and then the first real war in forty years breaks out between two small countries. Sponsors pull out of the season, and everyone has to decide whether Theatre should go off the air: Dark or Play. Militia wears a patch for one side the next morning and collects a kit warning. Nobody can admit where they're from, so the arguments are full of guessing. Militia is named a trainer, loses the job overnight, and gets it back.

On the Day of the Ban, Slier bars Martin, the Eagles' captain, from Hydra's boathouse after a public slanging match. Martin mentions "alts" and casually hands Militia ladder access. Randy warns him off, and he goes anyway. Slier finds him in the ladder hall and benches him. Militia flips his badge to "free agent" and stays, storms the boathouse, and insults his captain in front of the whole club. His access is cut room by room. "Please, sir," he says on the harbour wall. Slier walks past him to the Atrium lights.

Martin offers him a guest spot with the Eagles. Division argues for him once he calms down. Upbringer, the diplomat whose family remembers a real war, watches. In his long confession to Slier, Militia hears it straight: "You follow yourself, not your club, and you lie too much. An undisciplined player, not a traitor."

Ninja, once a rig technician, takes him to the maintenance bay and shows him a ghost calibration, the one smurf trick that works at Castellan. If he's caught, he gets a lifetime ban. A first-year called Mangy walks into Hydra's boathouse to keep Militia's friendships alive, while the real Militia, through Upbringer, makes the Wolf Federation's youth squad, the biggest club on the island. His acceptance comes mid-scrim while he's losing a naval battle and reading his messages.

Division catches Mangy within a week. "I know you'll use this information to be more careful, and I'm telling you anyway because I'll always be one step ahead." The secret stays between Division and Upbringer, but Militia is kicked from Hydra and the Wolves at once, and Martin signs him to the Eagles. Within days the Wolves' sponsor pulls its money and the three squads split. Militia's mess has broken up the most famous club at Castellan, and he never sees it.

Book Two: Eagles

The Eagles are loud and fun, sponsored, and invisible beyond Martin. Militia makes himself their diplomat, copying Upbringer, and gets into every club hall. At the Debate Club he meets Cryowolf, who talks about spies. He renames Mangy as Aguda and sends him to Redwolf, one of the three new clubs, where he befriends Geoff. He builds a second smurf, HappyHelper. Opulon turns him away from his French-speaking club and points him to the Rainmen, the café-kid club, where Ikki gets him in.

The Grand Project becomes a full scouting file on every future pro: tendencies, tells, friendships, weak spots. Militia thinks he's protecting the academy. The reader can see it's worth a fortune to any betting syndicate, and that it's a road map to unmasking every player at Castellan. Division finds Aguda and keeps quiet. Their friendship becomes real. Militia offers help to Templar, a nervous rookie, one last time. Then, alone, he records one more ghost calibration and calls it Sparky. Even Division doesn't know.

Book Three: Manifest Destiny

The players announce their first tournament, thirteen drafted teams of five, and the League agrees to broadcast it under handles because the war has emptied the schedule. Militia captains a team he calls Manifest Destiny and drafts Geoff and Ikki through his smurfs. He shows Division the Project. Then he turns it on Division and finds the youth-combine final Division threw for a syndicate years ago. Division takes everything to Upbringer.

Days before the opening, the Eagles drop Militia without explanation. Live, in the early rounds, Geoff and Ikki work out who Aguda and HappyHelper really are. Upbringer exposes the Project, and the collapse plays out to millions. Militia's drafted team decides what he is in front of the cameras, a few players stand up for him and pay for it, and the League cuts the feed: the first dead air in Theatre's history. Militia confesses everything to Upbringer in seven plain points before the League bans him for life.

Epilogue

The Mask Rule protects even the disgraced. The world knows the handle Militia, and nobody knows the man. He goes home. Over dinner his mother says she saw something on the news about an awful kid at that academy, someone called Militia. He agrees it sounds awful. Somewhere on a League server, a ghost calibration called Sparky still has a rating, and the adult open combine runs again in five years.
