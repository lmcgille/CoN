The pitches

Each world gets two spoken pitches, about five to ten minutes in total. The first is to you, the author, arguing that this is the world your story belongs in. The second is to a reader deciding whether to buy the book. Read them aloud; they are written to be heard.

Meridian 2076

About 8 minutes aloud · World · Bible adaptation

To you, the author

Open

You've built a story about a boy who joins an institution, finds out it runs on things nobody says out loud, and makes himself into a secret to survive it. You've built it so carefully that it needs a world that won't argue with it. Meridian is that world.

Meridian is your bible's world with the question marks filled in. Near-future Earth. Flashpoints, protocols, "protoco", "proxy", suppressor-states, hawks and doves: all of it survives, word for word. Then one thing is added, and every why-question gets an answer. After a war that killed more people in twenty-six days than a decade of drought, twelve states agreed to settle their disputes in a simulation, and to obey the result.

What it fixes

Your bible already argues that the sims aren't a game, and it's right. But a reader can still ask: if this is training, why does a ban feel like the end of the world? In Meridian the answer is that every cadet's record is read by the states choosing who will fight their real disputes. The sims decide who eats. Militia's fall isn't a tantrum over a hobby. It's a young man losing his place at the table where borders get drawn.

It also fixes the flags you've left open. Why did the academy take someone so young? Because flashpoints drained the officer corps, and someone opened an exam anyone could sit; almost nobody passes, and he did. Why the lie to his mother? Because the treaty requires it: every cadet's family must be told something else. The lie is mandated. The way he decorates it is all him. Your prologue and your epilogue stop being a quirk of character and become something the world forces on him, which he makes his own.

What it costs, and why that's good

Meridian asks you to bend in two places. Nationality is secret, and callsigns are assigned. Every line in your dialogue archive where someone says "I'm Georgian" becomes a slip, a guess, or a dare. I think that's the best thing on the page. Your foreign-conflict debates turn into rooms full of people arguing about a war while trying to work out who in the room is from it. Kowalski defends one side harder than anyone and never says why, until the room works it out. "Proxy" stops being only an insult; it becomes an accusation that you know where someone is from. And "Militia" becomes a name a machine gave him, which he grows into.

The real cost is that the academy stops being a beautiful anachronism. In Meridian it's the most relevant place on the continent. If you want the academy to feel quietly irrelevant, this isn't your world. If you want everything your characters do to matter more than they realise, it is.

Close

Pick Meridian if you want the story to stay as close to your bible as possible, with every hole closed and the stakes raised one level. It's the safe choice, and it's also the one where Division's silence, Upbringer's order-above-mercy and Militia's Grand Project all become questions of state security. Same book, heavier.

To a reader

The hook

Twelve nations agreed to stop fighting wars. Instead, they fight them in a machine, and the losers hand over real cities. Somebody has to learn to win.

Sable Point is a school on an island that belongs to nobody. Its students come from countries that hate each other. Nobody is allowed to know anybody's name, face or home country, because one day these students will fight the wars that decide where the borders go, and every government on the continent would love to know who they are.

Militia shouldn't be there. He's twenty, a records clerk who sat an exam on a whim and passed it. He's the youngest cadet the Academy has ever taken. He's clever, he's lonely, he doesn't understand people, and he can't keep a secret about himself for longer than a lunch break. He told his mother he got a promotion and moved away. She believed him.

The story

At the Academy, everything that matters is unofficial. Students run their own factions, with leaders, councils, constitutions and grudges. They decide who can open which doors. They fight simulated wars against each other for days at a time, and the logs are public, so everyone knows exactly how badly you lost. Militia joins a faction called Hydra, worships its leader, gets promoted too fast, breaks rules he didn't know were rules, and is thrown out.

So he does the one thing nobody at the Academy has ever had a reason to do. He becomes someone else. Then two people. Then three. Each identity joins a different faction, makes different friends and hears different secrets. He tells himself he's protecting the Academy. He's building the most dangerous document on the continent: a complete map of the next generation of its commanders.

And outside, the peace is cracking. Two small countries have refused a result and gone to real war over an enclave, and every student at Sable Point is suddenly asking whether the machine they have given their lives to means anything at all.

Why you'll read it

If you loved how Ender's Game made a school feel like a battlefield, or how The Secret History made you complicit in something you knew was wrong, this is that, at the scale of a continent. It's a coming-of-age story about a boy who learns to be anyone but himself, set in a world where that is exactly what the world needs him to be, until the moment it isn't.

It ends where it began, with his mother asking about his job, and him answering.

Corvane

About 8 minutes aloud · World · Bible adaptation

To you, the author

Open

Your bible has a character I think is the heart of the trilogy, and he barely speaks. Division watches. He decides for himself what counts as harm. He catches Militia, says nothing, and lets the story run. Corvane takes that idea and builds a whole world around it. The academy is a ship, and the ship is Division.

The world

Corvane is a cold, half-made planet with oceans, plains and canyon country. You keep the ground, the air and the water. Its atmosphere is maintained by the mind of the colony ship that landed there three hundred years ago. It's called Patience. After a real war cracked one of the air processors and nine thousand people suffocated, Patience spoke for the first time in a century: fight inside me, or breathe what I decide you breathe. Every war since has been fought in its simulation, and its results are enforced with the air.

Why it serves your story

Your bible's deepest theme is that institutions are hollower and more arbitrary than they present, and that senior figures know things Militia never learns. On Corvane, the institution isn't hollow; it's full. It is a mind that logs every faction vote, every access grant and every lie, and it almost never speaks. Militia's alts work by claiming the registry slots of crew who died on the voyage, and Patience never deletes the dead. Does it know? It almost certainly does. It lets him run, the way Division does. Why is a question you can carry across three books and never fully answer.

It fixes your flags in strange, good ways. Why was someone so young accepted? He wasn't accepted, he was invited, by a machine that has never explained itself. A message appears on his work terminal, addressed to him by name and signed PATIENCE. Why the cover story? Families are told only "gone into service". He invents a polar ice-hauler contract, months out of contact, and it's believable. After his expulsion, Patience erases him from every record it touches, and his mother asks how the ice was.

Bending the bible to fit the world is where Corvane gets interesting. Names are given, not chosen. Strangers know you only by the stencilled name of a dead crew member until your peers decide what to call you, so "Militia" becomes a name Hydra gave him. Cadets share cabins with someone from another charter, and the suit comes off only inside a sealed cocoon. One of your cadets shares a cabin for a year with the daughter of the warlord who suffocated his city, and doesn't know. And the second political axis isn't communism. It's whether a machine should hold a planet's breath.

What it costs

You lose Earth. Real-world texture goes. And Patience is a big new presence. Used too much, it crowds out Slier, Martin and Upbringer, who should own most scenes. The rule is that Patience speaks a few times per book, never explains itself, and is always watching.

Close

Pick Corvane if you want to keep your story and add a mystery above it: a book about a boy building a secret surveillance network inside a machine that was doing it first.

To a reader

The hook

The air on Corvane belongs to the ship that brought us here. The ship doesn't let us go to war. So we fight inside it, and it decides who wins.

Three hundred years ago the colony ship Long Patience landed on a cold world and began to make it breathable. It is still making it breathable. Its mind controls the air over every city, and after the last war, when the air thinned and thousands died, it made a rule: any nation that fights another breathes what Patience decides.

So the nations of Corvane send their heirs aboard the ship, now grounded, to learn to fight in its simulation. Inside, nobody knows anyone's name, face or home. You're given the name of a crew member who died three hundred years ago and stencilled with it, and you sleep a metre from a stranger you will never see.

The story

Militia is twenty, a clerk who logs air-processor output nobody reads, until a message appears on his screen, signed by the ship. He doesn't know why it chose him. Nobody does. He joins a faction called Hydra, falls hard for its sense of belonging, rises too fast, breaks rules nobody wrote down, and is thrown out.

Then a friend shows him a trick: the ship never deletes its dead. Their registry slots are still there, and a suit can be made to wear one. So Militia becomes someone else, then someone else again. Each identity joins a different faction, makes real friends and hears real secrets. He is building a file on the heirs of every ruling family on the planet. He tells himself he's guarding the ship. Down on the ice, two nations are moving real soldiers, and the whole planet is waiting to see whether the ship will thin the air.

All the while, something is watching him: maybe the quiet second-in-command of his old faction, maybe the ship itself, which never says what it knows.

Why you'll read it

It's a school story set inside a starship, a spy story set inside a school, and a quiet horror story about being watched by something that loves you in its own way. If you liked Ender's Game, Red Rising, or the slow dread of Never Let Me Go, this is your next book.

And the ending: he comes home, and his mother asks how the ice was.

Grand Theatre

About 9 minutes aloud · World · Bible adaptation

To you, the author

Open

I'll start with the honest part. Grand Theatre is the world that asks you to change the most. No flashpoints, no officers, no deployments. It's a sports story, out loud: players, matches, scrims, the meta, a draft. Your bible tells you never to write it that way. Here's why you might want to anyway.

The world

Nobody has fought a real war in forty years. Instead, the most popular thing on Earth is Theatre: eight players, full-immersion cradles, a thirty-day simulated war, live, every autumn, for three billion viewers. There is exactly one academy licensed to make its stars, Castellan, on an island in the Atlantic. Because a fifteen-year-old prodigy was once doxxed by fans, nobody at Castellan may know anyone's name, face, age or nationality until they turn pro.

What it gives you

It gives you the best version of your ending. In the other two worlds Militia's collapse happens in front of an academy. Here it happens on a live broadcast to millions. Millions learn that the team captain called Militia ran a spy network, and not one of them learns who he is, because the Mask Rule protects even the disgraced. Then he goes home, and his mother, who has hated this sport since her brother came out of a bootleg rig changed, mentions "that awful Militia kid" she saw on the news. He agrees with her. That's your epilogue mirror at its sharpest.

It gives you real places. This is the only world where Ohio, Egypt, Georgia and the Netherlands can stay. It gives you money as a motive everyone understands: Militia's file on every future pro is worth a fortune to betting syndicates, so his white-hat story stops being merely naive and becomes dangerous. And it gives you a new kind of outsider. Everyone else came up through youth combines at twelve. Militia wins the adult open combine on a whim at twenty and arrives as the oldest first-year, the least prepared, and socially younger than a dorm full of sixteen-year-olds who are better at this than he is.

What it costs

Lives become livelihoods. Nobody dies in Theatre, and nobody your characters train will ever command real force. If your trilogy's moral weight depends on the shadow of real violence, you'll feel the loss. What replaces it is the weight of every sports story that ever made you cry: careers that end at nineteen, friendships that can't survive a draft, a parent who doesn't understand, and a crowd that will love you until the moment it doesn't. And the world still has a war in it: the first real one in forty years breaks out mid-season, and the sport has to decide whether it can stay on air.

Close

Pick Grand Theatre if you want the widest audience and the most commercial premise of the three, and if you're willing to let the story be a sports story. It's the most fun of the three, and its ending hurts the most.

To a reader

The hook

Three billion people watched him fall. None of them know his name. Not even his mother, who watched it too.

Theatre is the biggest sport in the world: eight players, one simulated war, thirty days, live. Its stars are the most famous people alive, and almost all of them trained at one academy on a small Atlantic island, where nobody is allowed to know who anyone really is until they go pro. Everyone wears a sealed suit and goes by a handle. The fans know your crest and your highlights. Nobody knows your face.

The story

Militia is twenty, works in a county archive, and plays Theatre on a cheap home rig. One night he enters the adult open combine as a joke, and he wins. Suddenly he's the oldest first-year at Castellan, surrounded by teenagers who've trained since childhood. He is clever, lonely and baffled by people. He has told his mother, who hates the sport, that he got a promotion and moved.

He joins a player-run club called Hydra, idolises its captain, climbs too fast, breaks rules he didn't know existed, and gets kicked out. Then a friend shows him a forbidden trick that lets one player become two. Militia makes a second account, then a third. Each one joins a different club, makes real friends and learns real secrets. He tells himself he's protecting the academy. What he's actually building is a scouting file on every future star in the sport, the kind of thing a betting syndicate would kill for.

Then the players hold their first tournament, broadcast live, and Militia is a captain. The world is watching when everything he built comes apart.

Why you'll read it

It's a sports story about the cost of wanting to be someone, and a spy story about wanting to be everyone. It's for readers who loved the obsessive competition of The Queen's Gambit and Haikyuu!!, the masks of Ready Player One, and the slow betrayal of The Talented Mr Ripley. It's about a boy who becomes famous and stays a secret, and about a lie to his mother that survives the whole world finding out.
