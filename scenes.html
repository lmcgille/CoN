One scene, three worlds

The evening of 10/19, from the Story Bible's locked beats: Slier's punishment, the free-agent switch, the storm-in, the expulsion, and "please, sir". Each version follows its world's rules, so you can hear how each one reads on the page.

These are drafts built from the bible's beat notes. The Dialogue Archive's captured lines aren't in the v15 file, so the dialogue is placeholder except where the bible quotes it.

Meridian 2076

Sable Point · the League building, then House Ash · Bible adaptation

The League building smelled of new carpet and old coffee, and it was louder than anywhere else on Sable Point because nobody here was pretending to study. Militia had found a seat near the back, under the standings board, where the purple trim on his shoulders would be hard to see from the door.

It was not hard enough to see. Slier came in at ten past five and walked straight to him without looking at the board.

"Your trainer role is suspended for a month." The modulator flattened Slier's voice into something calm, but it couldn't do anything about how fast he was speaking. "You're moved to Hydra Blue. You leave this building now. And you stop talking to Martin."

Militia started to say something about Martin. Slier was already walking back to the door.

He watched the door close. Then he lifted his forearm and tapped the panel, and the little holographic plate that said HYDRA rose and hung in the air over his wrist. Underneath it was a toggle he had never touched, for cadets between factions. Free agent. There were six of them in the room already. Nobody looked at free agents.

He pressed it. The purple went grey. Nothing else happened, and that was the strangest part: the building just let him stay.

· · ·

By the time he crossed the quad to House Ash he had built the whole argument. He had not broken the constitution. He had read the constitution. There was nothing in it about the League, nothing about Martin, nothing about who you were allowed to sit next to. Slier had made up a rule on the spot because he was angry about something else. Militia could prove it. He had the clauses in his head.

The Cistern's door opened for him. He noticed that later: the Blue grant hadn't been set up yet, so the door still thought he was Hydra.

The common room was full. He didn't sit down. He asked where Slier was, and then he asked again louder, and somebody laughed, and he asked a third time in a voice he had never heard himself use. Over by the window a tall cadet in a scarred grey helmet was leaning against the wall with his arms folded. Upbringer, though Militia did not yet understand what that name meant. He didn't move.

Slier came in already knowing. Militia could tell by the way he stopped in the doorway, the way his visor turned once round the room before it settled on him.

Militia said the thing about the constitution. Then he said a worse thing, about Slier, about cowards who make up rules, and somebody near the fireplace said "oh," very quietly.

Slier didn't raise his voice. "Take him out."

Two of the council took his arms. They weren't rough about it, which was somehow worse. As they walked him to the door he felt his wrist buzz, then buzz again: grants being pulled, one after another. Common room. Training rooms. Game room. The Cistern's outer door closed behind him, and when he turned and touched it, it stayed shut.

· · ·

He deleted his League access standing on the path, as if it were a bargaining chip, as if anyone would see.

At twenty to eight the Cistern's door opened and Slier came out alone. Militia knew the helmet before he knew he was moving.

"Please, sir." He hadn't planned the sir. "Please. You said you were here to support me in anything I need. You said that."

Slier kept walking. His visor stayed pointed at the path ahead.

"Please, sir."

The tram bell rang down by the harbour. Slier turned the corner by the Field and was gone. Militia stood on the gravel with his arms at his sides. There was no one on the whole island he was allowed to tell what had happened, and no one at home who knew he was here.

Corvane

The Keel · the League compartment, then the old grow-bay · Bible adaptation

The League held the old cargo hold on Deck Four, and it was the only compartment on the ship that was ever warm, because so many suits were packed into it. Militia had found a crate near the back, under the standings, where nobody would read his rank board.

Slier read it from the hatch.

He crossed the hold without hurrying. The cold came off him in a little haze where his suit met the warmer air. "Trainer role, suspended. One month. Hydra Blue." The suit radio took every edge off his voice, so there was nothing left in it but the words. "You leave now. And you stop talking to the Brokerage boy."

Then he was gone, and the hatch sighed shut behind him.

Militia lifted his forearm. The hologram rose over his wrist: the purple grow-light emblem, his crew number underneath it in small type. And under that, a choice he had never used: Unaffiliated.

He chose it. The emblem greyed. Somewhere in the walls, he knew, the ship wrote it down.

· · ·

The walk back to the grow-bay took eleven minutes, along the spine corridor where the lights still ran on the voyage schedule. By the second minute he had the argument. By the sixth he had the counter-arguments, and by the tenth he had answered all of them. Nothing he had done was written anywhere. He knew Hydra's constitution better than anyone in Hydra. The rule about Martin had been invented in that hold, in that minute, because Slier was angry about something else.

The grow-bay hatch opened for him. The Blue grant hadn't caught up yet.

The bay was full and purple and humid with the smell of the old soil racks. He didn't sit. He said Slier's name and nobody answered, so he said it again, louder, and the third time the voice came out of him wrong, cracked and too high, and the suit radio carried every bit of it. A cadet against the far rack, grey helmet etched with fine lines like frost, watched him with his arms folded and didn't move.

Slier came in already knowing. Militia said the thing about the constitution. Then he said the thing about cowards.

"Out," Slier said.

Two of the council walked him to the hatch. Behind him the grants came off one by one, and on this ship you could hear it: every hatch in Hydra's compartments gave a soft double click as it re-locked against him. The bay. The training rooms. The little game room with the coin bot. The outer hatch closed in front of his visor, and when he touched it, it stayed closed. It had taken less than a second.

· · ·

He deleted his League grant standing in the corridor. It felt like an offering. There was nobody to offer it to.

Slier came out an hour later. Militia was still there.

"Please, sir." The sir surprised them both. "You said you were here to support me in anything I need. You said it. In the Commons. I remember the day."

Slier walked past him and down the spine, and did not turn his head.

"Please, sir."

The corridor lights dimmed into the ship's night cycle, as they had every evening for three hundred years. Somewhere in the walls the ship logged that a cadet was standing alone outside a hatch that would not open. It said nothing. It never did.

Grand Theatre

Castellan · the ladder hall, then Hydra's boathouse · Bible adaptation

The ladder hall was where the real season happened. The official House standings ran on the big screen in the Atrium for the cameras. Down here, on a wall of cheap monitors, was the players' ladder, the one the scouts actually read. Militia had found a beanbag in the back row, where his purple club trim would get lost in the glare.

It didn't. Slier came down the stairs at ten past five, captain's band bright on his chest plate, and came straight to him.

"You're off the trainer list for a month. You're down to Blue. You leave the ladder hall now." The helmet speaker made him sound almost bored. Militia knew he wasn't. "And stop hanging around with Martin."

He went back up the stairs. Somebody two rows down whistled the drawn-out note that means somebody's in trouble.

Militia tapped his forearm. His club badge floated up over his wrist, HYDRA in purple, and under it the little grey toggle every first-year noticed and nobody used. Free agent. There were six free agents on the ladder right now. Nobody looked twice at free agents. They were just players between clubs, waiting for a call.

He hit it. The purple went grey. The ladder hall didn't care.

· · ·

He walked to the boathouse along the harbour wall with the wind coming off the Atlantic, and by the time he got there he had the whole case. Hydra's constitution was four pages long, and he'd read it twice. Nothing in it said a player couldn't go on the ladder. Nothing said who you could eat lunch with. Slier was making it up because Martin had embarrassed him in the Atrium that morning, and everybody knew it.

The boathouse door opened for him. Nobody had set up his Blue access yet.

The common room was packed. Somebody's scrim was on the big screen with the sound off. He didn't sit. He asked for Slier, then asked louder, and the third time his helmet speaker clipped and the whole room heard his voice break. Over by the trophy shelf, a tall player with a frost-etched visor and an emblem Militia didn't recognise watched with his arms crossed. He didn't move.

Slier came in already knowing. Militia said the thing about the constitution. Then he said the other thing, the one with "coward" in it, and the room went quiet the way a crowd goes quiet when a player gets sent off.

"Get him out," Slier said.

Two of the council walked him out by the elbows. His wrist buzzed, then buzzed again: the captain pulling his access room by room. The boathouse door shut. When he touched it, nothing happened. His draft stock, he thought, absurdly. Somebody somewhere was already updating his draft stock.

· · ·

He deleted his ladder account on the harbour wall. He wasn't sure who it was for.

Slier came out a little before eight. Militia stepped into his way, then out of it.

"Please, sir." He'd never called anyone sir in his life. "Please. You told me you were here to support me in anything I need. You said that."

Slier went past him toward the Atrium lights.

"Please, sir."

On the big screen across the water a highlight reel was playing for nobody, somebody's perfect pincer from last season, over and over. He stood there and watched it until the harbour lights came on. His mother would be watching something on television right now. She thought he was in Chicago.
