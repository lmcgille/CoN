Meridian 2076

Fifty years after the Long Drought broke the old alliances, the seabed of the Kessarine Shelf has become dry land, contested farmland and the richest lithium flats on the planet. Twelve successor states want it. Only one of them can write the map.

The world

Setting, geography, and the people your characters will be playing.

In the 2040s the rains stopped falling where the old capitals expected them. The Drought lasted eleven years, and by the time it broke, the treaties that had held a continent together were paper. What came after was not one country but a dozen: city-states that kept their water plants running, rump republics, a corporate protectorate, and one disciplined military directorate that never demobilised.

The prize is the Kessarine Shelf — a broad continental margin left high and dry when engineered dams and a decade of evaporation pulled the inland sea back two hundred kilometres. The exposed seabed is flat, salt-crusted and absurdly rich: lithium brines, rare-earth nodules, and soil that turns black and fertile once the salt is flushed. Around it sit old coastal cities now stranded inland, a ragged new coastline of shallow lagoons, and to the south the cold, deep Varn Trench, where submarines can disappear for weeks.

Each player commands one successor state: a capital, a handful of cities, and a scatter of outland districts. Victory goes to whoever holds enough of the map's Mandate — the weighted value of cities, districts and three strategic sites (the Halcyon Desalination Works, the Ostrel Lithium Flats, and the Sable Point Orbital Uplink) — at a daily count.

Geography that shapes the war

The Salt Flats

Dead-flat open ground across the centre of the Shelf. Heavy armour runs at full speed and full strength here; infantry caught in the open die. This is where tank wars happen.

The Sunken Cities

Old coastal metropolises left stranded inland. Dense urban terrain that eats armour and protects dug-in infantry. Every major campaign ends in one of them.

The Corran Highlands

Mountain spine in the north. Vehicles crawl and fight badly; Highland Rangers thrive. A natural wall for whoever owns it.

The Lagoon Coast

The new coastline: shallow, warm, mined easily. Small warships rule; submersibles are half-blind and fragile here. Ships heal quickly in friendly shallows.

The Varn Trench

Deep cold water south of the Shelf. Submersibles are at their deadliest; littoral cutters lose most of their strength and hull integrity in the swell.

The Mangrove Belt

Jungle-thick reclaimed wetland in the east. Slow for everyone, murderous for vehicles, a gift to defenders.

Upkeep is the silent killer. Every unit keeps drawing a slice of what it cost, every day, plus Power capacity. A state that raises a giant army on day two has spent its economy for the rest of the war. Shortages of Rations or Scrip hit Cohesion — and Cohesion multiplies everything.

Territory

Core cities

Your homeland. Full output, full Cohesion, can build anything. The capital boosts Cohesion in nearby districts.

Held cities

Conquered and occupied. A quarter of normal output. They begin at very low Cohesion and are the cities that rise against you.

Chartered cities

A held city you have formally integrated by building a Civic Charter (costs Cohort). Half output, can raise units. Only worth it behind the lines.

Outland districts

The countryside between cities. Low output unless you build an Extractor on a resource deposit. Districts never revolt.

Cohesion and unrest

Every city and district has Cohesion from 0–100. It multiplies output, speeds or slows construction, grows population, and decides whether a city stays yours. Cohesion drifts each day toward a target set by: distance from your capital, the number of wars you are fighting, enemy troops nearby, shortages, civilian casualties, enemy agents, and the buildings you have put there.

A held city below about a third Cohesion can rise. A freshly taken city starts near a quarter. Once a day, at an unpredictable hour, each city rolls. If it rises and there are no troops in it, it simply becomes a Free Zone — owned by the rebel faction, the Unbound, who raise militia and wander. If you left even one infantry unit there, your garrison fights the rising with a home advantage and usually wins.

Rules of battle

The handful of rules that create every surprise in this world.

Exchanges

When two forces touch, the first exchange resolves instantly; after that, one every 30 minutes until a side dies, withdraws, or breaks contact. Each exchange compares the attacker's rating against the defender's hit class. A unit with a huge anti-armour rating does almost nothing to infantry.

Hold-points and digging in

Every city and district has a hold-point. A force parked on it digs in automatically: it takes much less damage and does not shoot back — it just absorbs. A force sitting near the point, not on it, gets no protection and trades blows every exchange.

Battlegroups, flights, flotillas

Ground battlegroups fight at full strength up to 12 units; air flights and naval flotillas up to 4. Past that each extra unit is worth less, then nothing, then actively drags the group down. Two groups of 12 beat one group of 24.

Lines

Inside a group, incoming damage is shared by line. Vanguard units soak most of it, Mainline units some, Rearguard units very little. You cannot choose — the unit type decides. An all-rearguard group (artillery, launchers) melts.

Reach and stand-off fire

Artillery, rocket arrays and some ships fire from stand-off range. A target without similar reach cannot answer. Fire one exchange, pull back, wait out the half-hour, step in again: the target bleeds and never touches you.

Only infantry claims

A city or district changes hands only when a claiming unit reaches its hold-point. Tanks, artillery, aircraft, ships, Wraith teams and contract mercenaries can destroy everything and claim nothing.

High air and low air

Jets fly high and are seen and shot by area missile batteries. Rotorcraft fly low, nap-of-the-earth — under Spire and Aegis coverage entirely. Only short-range point-defence (Lancets, frigates, interceptors) touches them.

Point defence vs umbrella

Air defence inside the targeted group fires at every incoming aircraft or missile, with no cooldown. Air defence merely covering a nearby group fires once, then recharges — so a salvo can saturate it.

Sensors and stealth

Line of sight shows that something is there; unidentified formations show as masked until scouted or engaged. Sensor masts and Overwatch aircraft see emissions at range. Stealth units are invisible until they fire or a matching revealer (ground, air or deep) is near.

Shallow and deep water

The Lagoon Coast is shallows; the Trench and open ocean are deep. Cutters thrive in shallows and wither in deep water. Submersibles are the reverse — fragile and blunted in shallows, lethal in the deep.

Transports

Ground units crossing water convert into transports at a Drydock. Transports cannot fight and barely float. The boarding and landing windows leave a force helpless for hours. Marines and Striders land on any beach.

Aerodromes

Every aircraft flies from a home field, returns to rearm, and is a sitting duck on the ground. Destroy an Aerodrome and every plane based there is grounded until repairs finish.

Hit classes

What a unit is "hit as" decides which of the attacker's ratings applies. The right-hand column is computed from the roster below.

Chronicle: The Shelf War

A complete war, told start to finish. Every defeat in it comes from a rule on this page.

Days 1–3Two kinds of patience

Marshal Ilse Kadrova did what Directorate marshals are trained to do: she spent her first morning on foundries, her first afternoon on tanks, and by the first evening she had two Bastion battlegroups idling on the Ostrel border with their engines warm. The Ostrel Communes had a militia, a lithium flat, and a Speaker who still believed in arbitration.

Four hundred kilometres west, Premier Tobias Wren of the Concord of Harrow built nothing that could shoot. He built a second foundry, a research campus, an aerodrome. His generals sent him memos. He did not read them. Concord arms were the best on the continent and the most expensive, and Wren had done the arithmetic: an army raised on day two would eat his economy for a month.

On the coast, Admiral Nkem Odu of the Saltmarch League raised a Hunter destroyer and a Warden frigate and parked them in the warm shallows off her capital. They were not exciting ships. That was rather the point.

Days 4–7The flats

Kadrova's armour crossed the Ostrel border on the salt flats, which is exactly where Bastions are meant to fight. The Communes' militia stood on open ground and died there. In three days the Directorate took four cities and the lithium flats. Her staff drew the new border in red on the situation map and the marshal had the map photographed for the evening broadcast.

She did not leave garrisons. Garrisons were infantry, infantry was slow, and the Bastions were already rolling toward the next city. Each captured city got the flag and a press release. None of them got a single soldier.

Day 8Nothing on the scope

Wren's first combat unit was not a tank. It was a flight of four Mantis hunters, rotorcraft built for one job, and Wren's air staff flew them out of the Harrow aerodrome at dawn, low over the Mangrove Belt, following the riverbeds.

Kadrova's lead battlegroup — five Bastions, two Siege Crawlers, nothing else — was refuelling on the flats east of Ostrel. Her two Spire batteries were a district behind, covering the column. The Spires' operators watched their scopes the entire time and saw nothing at all, because there was nothing to see: Mantis hunters fly below the horizon that area batteries watch.

The first exchange took two Bastions. The column had no answer — Bastions carry nothing that can elevate onto a rotorcraft — and so the hunters simply stayed. Half an hour later, another exchange. Then another. By noon the battlegroup was wreckage on the salt, and the Spire crews filed a report that said, accurately, that no hostile aircraft had entered their coverage.

"Five tanks. Two batteries watching them. And they died without firing a shot." — Directorate after-action review, Day 8

Days 9–11The wrong umbrella

Kadrova's answer was the Directorate answer: more air defence. She ordered six more Spire batteries and an Aegis dome, the best long-range interceptor her bloc could field. Every one of them was blind to low air. Wren's hunters kept killing tanks, and the new batteries kept reporting clear skies. A single Lancet point-defence vehicle in each column — cheap, short-ranged, ugly — would have shredded the Mantis flight. She had none. Her doctrine called them a "rear-area luxury."

Meanwhile Admiral Odu signed a compact with Wren. The Saltmarch League and the Concord would count their Mandate together. Kadrova now had two enemies, one on her flank and one on the sea.

Day 12The flotilla

Kadrova still had an air force — a small one, the Directorate's weakest arm, but real: four Kestrel strike jets and two Atlas bombers. Her intelligence reported Odu's flotilla anchored in the shallows off Saltmarch: a Hunter destroyer, two Monitor cruisers, and a fourth hull the analysts logged as "escort, minor." Monitors were the most dangerous surface ships on the coast and terrible at defending themselves from the air. It looked like the best target of the war.

The "minor escort" was the Warden frigate. A Warden is almost nothing as a warship — it barely scratches other ships — but it is a flying-saucer of radar and point-defence missiles, and when it sails inside a flotilla it fires on every single aircraft that attacks that flotilla, with no pause between targets. The Monitors had been bait without anyone intending it.

The strike went in at 0300. The first exchange resolved before the Kestrels released. Of six aircraft, two reached their launch point. One returned to base. The Monitors took a scratch.

"I sent my entire air force at three ships and lost my entire air force to the fourth." — Marshal Kadrova, private diary

Days 13–15The cities rise

Behind Kadrova's lines, the four Ostrel cities she had taken in a week had been sitting at a quarter Cohesion ever since — far from her capital, full of people who did not want her, and empty of soldiers. On the night of Day 13 two of them rose in the same hour. There was nothing in them to fight the rising, so the Unbound simply took them. By Day 15 there was a rebel enclave sitting on her main supply corridor, raising militia of its own, and she had to turn a battlegroup around to deal with it.

Days 16–19The hourglass

Kadrova concentrated. Everything she had left went into a single enormous battlegroup — twenty-two units, tanks, crawlers and infantry together — aimed at the Concord fortress city of Lisle. On the map it was terrifying. In practice, past twelve units every new vehicle made the group fight worse, not better; at twenty-two, it fought at about two-thirds strength and moved at a crawl.

Wren put Railjack tank hunters and infantry on Lisle's hold-point, where they dug in and simply absorbed. Two districts back he put three Hailstorm arrays. Every half hour the arrays rolled forward, fired one exchange into the Directorate column from beyond its reach, and rolled back before the column could close. Twelve hours of this. The column never touched them. When it finally reached Lisle at half strength, it hit dug-in defenders who took a fraction of the damage and kept absorbing. The assault broke on the second day.

Day 21The crossing

Desperate for a second front, Kadrova embarked her last reserve — two battlegroups of infantry and crawlers — at her southern drydock to land behind the Saltmarch coast. To save time she sent them straight across the Varn Trench, deep water, without a destroyer escort, because her destroyers were still being built.

Two Saltmarch Lamprey submersibles had been sitting in the Trench for nine days, invisible, waiting for exactly that. Transports do not fight back. The Lampreys surfaced from nothing, the first exchange resolved, and the reserve was gone before the Directorate even saw what had hit it.

Days 22–26Grounded

Wren's last move was the cheapest. Concord glide missiles hit the Directorate's two remaining aerodromes on the same night. Every replacement aircraft Kadrova had rebuilt since Day 12 was parked on those fields; every one of them was grounded or destroyed. With no air cover at all, her remaining columns became targets for the Mantis flights, and then for Atlas bombers flying unopposed.

By Day 26 the Directorate's Mandate had collapsed below the Concord–League compact's, and the war ended at the next daily count.

Why Kadrova lost

  1. Armour with no point-defence. Bastions cannot touch rotorcraft. One Lancet per column would have changed Day 8.
  2. The wrong air defence. Spire batteries and Aegis domes cannot see low air. She bought the expensive answer to a different question.
  3. Attacking an escorted flotilla from the air. A Warden inside the flotilla fires at every aircraft, every exchange. The target that looked weakest was protected by the hull that looked least important.
  4. No garrisons. Fast conquests at low Cohesion, left empty, rose and became an enemy behind her own lines.
  5. The overstuffed battlegroup. Twenty-two units fighting as one at reduced strength, slowly, into stand-off fire it could never answer.
  6. Assaulting a dug-in hold-point. The defender absorbed; she paid for every exchange.
  7. Unescorted transports over deep water. Submersibles in their element against targets that cannot shoot back.
  8. Planes on the ground. Aircraft parked within missile range are not an air force, they are a target list.

And why Wren won: he spent the first week on economy, built counters to what his enemy actually fielded, let defence do the absorbing, and only fought in ways his enemy could not answer.
