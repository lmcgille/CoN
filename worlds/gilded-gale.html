The Gilded Gale

In 1887 a drilling crew on Lindqvist Island struck phlogiston — a gas lighter than anything in nature, that burns hotter than coal and lifts iron into the sky. Within a decade every power on the Aurelian Sea had built an air fleet. Within two, they wanted the wells.

9 powers 8 hit classes   units Brigades of 14 · squadrons & flotillas of 6 An engagement every two hours

The world

An inland sea, a hundred islands, and a gas that changed everything.

The Aurelian Sea is enclosed: a great warm basin ringed by continental powers and studded with islands. Before phlogiston it was a backwater of fishing fleets and wine. After, it became the most valuable water in the world, because the gas only seeps up through the volcanic islands at its centre — Lindqvist, Saint Orme, the Embers — and nowhere else.

Phlogiston fills the envelopes of dirigibles, drives the turbines of the new aeroplanes, and is the secret of the rocket. Every power that wants an air fleet needs it, and every power has an air fleet.

Each player governs one power: a capital, a few industrial cities, and provinces. Victory is Ascendancy, assessed each Sunday by the Congress of Calvessa: the weighted value of cities, provinces and the three Wellheads.

Wide grassland on the northern shore. Landships at full steam; infantry in the open is cut down.

Cobbles, canals, cathedral squares. Landships grind to a crawl and lose their punch; riflemen in cellars hold for weeks.

High, snowy, and owned by whoever has Jägers.

Marsh and reed. Slow for everyone, impassable for heavy machines.

Coastal waters

The open centre of the sea. Submersibles hunt; small boats founder in the swell.

Coal is bread. The people heat with it and the army runs on it. A power that mobilises everything in the first week spends the winter rationing coal — and cold cities lose Public Spirit, which slows every mill and every shipyard.

Territory

Home cities

Full production; can build everything. The capital lifts Public Spirit in its region.

Occupied cities

A quarter production, Public Spirit near the floor. These are the cities that declare a Commune.

Annexed cities

Occupied cities brought in by an Act of Union (costs Hands). Half production; can raise regiments.

Provinces

Countryside. Little output unless you sink a mine or well. Provinces do not rise.

Public Spirit and the Commune

Every city and province has Public Spirit. It drifts each morning toward a level set by distance from the capital, the number of wars, enemy regiments near, shortages of coal or Marks, shelling casualties and foreign agitators — and the hospitals, barracks and shelters you build.

An occupied city below a third Public Spirit may declare a Commune. Newly taken cities start at a quarter. Each day, at an hour no one can predict, the barricades go up or they don't. If no regiment is quartered there, the city simply joins the Commune — the sea-wide revolutionary movement, which raises its own militia and marches where it likes. One quartered regiment usually clears the barricades.

Rules of battle

The regulations of war on the Aurelian Sea.

Engagements

The first exchange of fire comes the moment forces meet; another every two hours after. Each weapon's effect depends on the target's hit class: an anti-landship ram-gun barely troubles riflemen.

The square

Every city and province has a square — the town hall, the crossroads. A regiment holding it is entrenched: it takes far less fire and does not return any. A regiment beside the square, not on it, trades volleys every engagement.

Brigades, squadrons, flotillas

A brigade fights at full effect up to 14 companies; an air squadron or flotilla up to 6. Above that, each addition is worth less, then nothing, then less than nothing. A formation travels at its slowest member's pace.

Firing line, support, train

Fire on a formation falls hardest on the firing line, less on support, least on the train. The unit's type decides where it marches.

Indirect fire

Field guns, rocket batteries and battleships fire from range. A target that can't reach back can't fire back. Fire, withdraw, wait two hours, fire again.

Raising the flag

A city or province changes hands only when flag-raising infantry reaches the square. Landships, guns, aircraft, ships, Saboteurs and Free Companies cannot raise a flag.

Altitude and the hedgerow

Aeroplanes and dirigibles fly high, inside the arc of searchlight-rocket batteries. Autogyros and ornithopters fly at hedgerow height — below the batteries' arc entirely. Only quick-firing pom-poms, arclight frigates and fighters can touch them.

Guns in the brigade vs guns nearby

Anti-aircraft guns in the targeted formation fire on every attacker and rocket, without pause. Guns near it fire, then reload — so a big enough raid swamps them.

Reconnaissance and camouflage

Pickets see what's near; distant formations are only "dust on the road" until a scout identifies them. Listening masts and observation dirigibles track engine noise at range. Camouflaged units — saboteurs, ghost zeppelins, submersibles — are unseen until they fire or a matching detector approaches.

Coastal and deep water

Launches and pickets love the coast and founder in the Basin. Submersibles are the reverse: half-blind and fragile near shore, lethal in the deep.

Transports

Regiments crossing water embark at a naval yard. Transports are unarmed and fragile, and embarking or landing leaves a regiment helpless for hours. Sea Fencibles and amphibian tractors land on any beach.

The aerodrome

Aircraft fly from a home aerodrome and return to refuel. Moored aircraft are helpless. Wreck the aerodrome and its fleet is grounded until rebuilt.

Hit classes

What a target is hit as decides which of the attacker's figures applies.

The three schools

Every general staff on the sea follows one of three schools of war.

The Foundry School

Cheap, early, steel-heavy. Landships and Congreve arrays in the first week, cheaply built and lightly armoured. Foundry air arms are thin, so Foundry powers trust searchlight-rocket batteries for air defence — which cannot see a gyro at hedgerow height.

The Aerial School

Expensive, sturdy, slow to start, rules the sky. Sky-Hussars, tankbuster ornithopters, observation dirigibles and arc towers arrive early. The Aerial powers build industry first and fight from above.

Chronicle: The Wellhead War

From the dispatches of Miss Harriet Quayle, war correspondent of the Calvessa Courier, with the occasional intercepted telegram.

Halvard mobilises; Wyndham builds factories

CALVESSA, FIRST WEEK. — The Lord-Marshal of Halvard does not believe in waiting. Within two days of the Lindqvist crisis his works yards were turning out landships — great riveted fortresses on caterpillar treads, each carrying four guns and a crew of forty — and by Saturday two full brigades of them stood on the Lindqvist frontier with steam up.

The Commonwealth of Wyndham, by contrast, has mobilised precisely nothing. The Prime Minister has laid the foundation stones of two manufactories and a polytechnic, and opened an aerodrome. Asked in the House when Wyndham would raise regiments, Mrs Ashcombe replied that she would raise them when they had been paid for. The opposition benches howled.

Here in Calvessa, the Grand Admiral has commissioned a torpedo-boat destroyer and a peculiar new vessel his officers call an arclight frigate: a narrow grey hull with almost no main battery, bristling with quick-firing pom-poms and searchlights. Both are moored in the harbour.

Lindqvist falls in five days

LINDQVIST, SECOND WEEK. — The Steppe Coast was made for landships, and the Grand Duchess's militia, drawn up in the open, never had a chance. I watched from a hill above Varberg as the Halvard machines rolled through the wheat. In five days the Lord-Marshal held four cities and the first Wellhead. Each time, a flag went up over the town hall; each time, the landships rolled on.

I asked a Halvard staff captain who would hold the towns. He looked at me as though I had asked who would hold the weather. "They are taken," he said. "We are going to the next one."

VARBERG FORD, SECOND WEEK. — I was there, and I still find it hard to believe. Six Wyndham ornithopters — the "tankbusters," flapping, insect-like things carrying shaped charges — came up the river valley at hedgerow height, so low their wingtips clipped the poplars. The two searchlight-rocket batteries covering the Halvard brigade swept the sky for an hour and saw nothing, because there was nothing in the sky: the machines were under it.

A landship's guns cannot elevate. The crews could only button up and wait. Every two hours the ornithopters returned. By evening the brigade was a line of burning hulls along the ford, and the battery commanders were still reporting the sky clear.

Halvard orders a forest of rocket batteries

HALVARD, THIRD WEEK. — The Lord-Marshal's answer has been to order eight more searchlight-rocket batteries and a galvanic arc tower, the most costly air defence in the Foundry inventory. Not one of them can see a machine at hedgerow height. A Wyndham pilot, shot down and interned here, told me, laughing, that a single pom-pom gun travelling with each brigade would have ended his squadron's career in an afternoon. Halvard's doctrine, I am told, regards pom-poms as "a nuisance to the march."

On Thursday, Calvessa and Wyndham signed the Treaty of the Two Harbours. Their Ascendancy will be assessed jointly.

Halvard air raid on Calvessa fleet repulsed — catastrophe for raiders

CALVESSA HARBOUR, FOURTH WEEK. — I watched this from the roof of the Courier. At three in the morning six Halvard Raider monoplanes — the Lord-Marshal's entire modern air arm — came over the harbour mole to strike the fleet at anchor: the destroyer, the battleship Serenissima, which everyone knows to be helpless against aircraft, and the arclight frigate, which no one had thought worth mentioning.

The frigate was moored among them. Every pom-pom she carried fired on every aeroplane that dived on any ship in the anchorage, and kept firing without a pause. The harbour lit up like a fairground. Two Raiders got close enough to see the battleship's flag. One made it home.

Communes declared in Lindqvist

VARBERG, FIFTH WEEK. — Two of the four Lindqvist cities Halvard took in the second week declared Communes on the same night. They had been cold, hungry and far from Halvard for a month, and not one Halvard regiment was quartered in either. The barricades went up at dusk and the red flags by morning. Now a Commune militia sits astride the Halvard railway, and the Lord-Marshal must send a brigade back to clear it.

The siege of Fort Ashcombe

WYNDHAM FRONTIER, SIXTH WEEK. — Halvard has gathered everything it has into a single enormous brigade — twenty-six companies of landships, walkers, guns and infantry — and thrown it at Fort Ashcombe. On parade it is a terrible sight. On the march it moves like a funeral, and its officers admit, privately, that half of it gets in the way of the other half.

Wyndham has put ram-gun carriages and line fusiliers into the fort's square, where they sit and endure. Two provinces back, three Congreve rocket arrays roll forward every two hours, fire one salvo into the great column from beyond the range of any Halvard gun, and roll back before the column can reach them. They have not lost a single crew. When the column arrived at the fort it was mauled and slow, and it spent itself on entrenched men who barely bled.

Halvard invasion fleet lost in the Basin

CALVESSA, SEVENTH WEEK. — The Lord-Marshal's last reserve, two brigades on transports, put out across the Deepwater Basin to land behind Calvessa. Halvard's own destroyers were still on the slips; the transports sailed alone. Two Calvessan nautilus boats had lain submerged in the Basin for nine days, waiting for precisely that. There are no survivors to interview.

Halvard aerodromes burn; the war is over

HALVARD, EIGHTH WEEK. — Galvanic torpedo-rockets fell on both Halvard aerodromes in a single night. Every aircraft the Lord-Marshal had rebuilt since the harbour raid was moored there. Without an air arm, and with rocket batteries that still cannot see a gyro, Halvard's last brigades were hunted across the steppe by ornithopters. At Sunday's assessment the Treaty powers' Ascendancy passed Halvard's. The Congress has called a peace conference. I am told the Lord-Marshal will not attend.

Miss Quayle's summing-up

  1. Landships with no pom-poms. They cannot fire upward. One quick-firer per brigade was all it needed.
  2. High-altitude defence against hedgerow raiders. Searchlight batteries and arc towers cannot see under their own arc.
  3. Raiding an escorted anchorage. Guns in the fleet fire on every attacker. The plainest ship was the deadliest.
  4. Cities left unquartered. Cold, distant and empty, they declared Communes behind his own lines.
  5. The twenty-six-company brigade. Too large to fight properly, too slow to catch the rockets.
  6. Storming an entrenched square. Men who do not fire back also do not bleed.
  7. Unescorted transports in deep water. That is what submersibles are for.
  8. Aircraft moored within rocket range. A grounded air arm is a target.

Chronicle II: The Treaty War

Miss Harriet Quayle's dispatches, a year after the Wellhead War.

Two treaties, one neutral

CALVESSA. — Halvard was partitioned after the Wellhead War, and the powers have settled into two treaties. The Northern Treaty: Mrs Ashcombe's Commonwealth of Wyndham and Landammann Ursula Brenn's Mountain Cantons. The Southern Treaty: Grand Admiral Marchetti's Calvessa and Lord Mayor Josiah Penhallow's Free City of Brassmere. Grand Duchess Elin Lindqvist, her wells restored, has joined neither. A two-power treaty must hold twice a single power's Ascendancy to win.

Brassmere seizes Wyndham port without firing a shot

PORT EDWIN, FIRST WEEK. — Under the old Transit Treaty, Brassmere's goods trains have run through Wyndham for a decade, with Brassmere fusiliers riding guard and billeted in Port Edwin's town hall. On Saturday the Lord Mayor declared war on the Commonwealth. His fusiliers were already in the square. The flag changed at noon.

Where are the Cantons?

WYNDHAM, SECOND WEEK. — Mrs Ashcombe has asked three times for the Cantons' Sky-Hussars over Port Edwin. Bern-am-See has not replied. The Cantons' mills run, their regiments drill, and their ambassador has stopped attending the Treaty council. They contribute nothing, but as long as they remain signatories the Treaty must reach double Ascendancy to win.

Congreve trains caught in a vice

MARSHES OF ABEL, THIRD WEEK. — Wyndham's rocket trains went to work again: roll in, fire, roll out. This time the Grand Admiral's brigade was bait. While the arrays withdrew, two flying columns of steam walkers and Motor Hussars, sent the night before, closed from east and west at once. An array can run from one enemy. Not from two. I saw the wreckage. It is spread over a mile of open marsh.

Twelve rockets over Wyndham aerodrome

WYNDHAM, FOURTH WEEK. — It is now plain that Calvessa has had an agent in the Commonwealth since the first days. That agent knew what Wyndham did not advertise: the main aerodrome sat under a galvanic arc tower, but its pom-pom guns had gone forward to Fort Ashcombe. On Tuesday two Calvessan destroyers fired twelve torpedo-rockets in one salvo. The arc tower brought down the first, recharged, brought down the fifth, recharged. The rest arrived. Most of a squadron was lost at its masts.

Fires in Calvessa; skies reported clear

CALVESSA, FIFTH WEEK. — For five nights the naval yards have burned and the batteries have reported nothing overhead. These are Wyndham's ghost zeppelins, and Calvessa's arc towers had not been fitted to see them. The Admiralty rushed the refit. On the sixth night, the towers saw them.

Brassmere harbour mined

BRASSMERE, FIFTH WEEK. — Two Wyndham destroyers entered the Brassmere roads by night and laid mines across the harbour mouth. Picket boats reported "faint hydrophone contacts, probably wreckage." Three torpedo launches and two transports were lost before the Lord Mayor closed his own port.

Miasma at Port Edwin

PORT EDWIN, SIXTH WEEK. — Wyndham had Port Edwin surrounded, and the Brassmere garrison was finished. Then the Lord Mayor used a Miasma Bomb. The siege broke. So did his own garrison, which was in the square. Port Edwin will not be habitable for a generation. Within two days every city of the Free City's dominion had turned against him. Three declared Communes on the same night.

Calvessa leaves the Treaty; Lindqvist's last-day harvest

CALVESSA, SEVENTH WEEK. — With Brassmere ruined, the Southern Treaty still needed double Ascendancy. On Saturday morning the Grand Admiral withdrew Calvessa from it, without informing the Lord Mayor. At Sunday's assessment Calvessa alone passed the single-power mark. The war is decided; the Congress will make its final count at midnight. Grand Duchess Lindqvist spent the day sending fusiliers and gyro-borne rifles into every empty province on Brassmere's collapsing border. She raised eleven flags before midnight.

Miss Quayle's second summing-up

  1. Transit rights are a cocked pistol. Fusiliers billeted in your square take it the moment their government declares.
  2. A silent signatory costs you. Every member raises the mark.
  3. Rocket trains can be trapped. Two columns, two directions, one moment.
  4. Agents compound. The one placed in the first week won the fourth.
  5. An arc tower can be swamped. It must recharge; pom-poms in the target do not.
  6. A clear sky is not proof. Fit the detector before the fires start.
  7. Mines sound like wreckage. Treat any unexplained contact in a harbour mouth as a minefield.
  8. Miasma is a slow suicide. It wins one siege and raises your own cities against you.
