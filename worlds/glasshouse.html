The Glasshouse

The Haverleigh Conservatory closed in 1961 and nobody turned the heating off. Sixty years later the great glasshouse is a sealed, steaming, overgrown world of its own — and every summer, the colonies inside it go to war over the nectar.

8 colonies 8 hit classes 38 castes Columns of 16 · flights of 7 · rafts of 4 A clash every quarter-hour

The world

A sealed glass continent, forty metres end to end, and the eight hive-minds that rule it.

Inside the conservatory the seasons never quite end. The palms have broken through the upper panes; the orchid beds have run wild; the ornamental ponds have become lakes with their own weather. And over six decades something odd has happened to the colonies. Isolated, overcrowded, and chemically marinated in sixty years of abandoned fertiliser and fungicide, each great nest has developed a single coordinating intelligence — a Matriarch — that thinks, plans and makes war.

There are eight Matriarchs. Each controls a Heartnest, a handful of satellite nests, and the forage-ground around them. Victory is Dominion: when the autumn cold comes through the broken panes, the colony that holds the most nests, ground and the three great blooms — the Corpse Lily, the Night Orchid and the Old Fig — overwinters. The others do not.

Terrain of the glasshouse

The Paving

Old flagstone walkways, flat and bare. Heavy beetles run at full strength; soft-bodied workers caught in the open are slaughtered.

The Nests

Tunnelled mounds and hollow stumps. Big beetles can barely turn in the galleries; dug-in workers can hold for days.

The Rockery

A collapsed alpine display. Cliff Weevils thrive; everything else stumbles.

The Fern Jungle

Dense understory. Slow for everyone, murderous for anything big.

The Lily Shallows

Pond margins choked with pads. Water striders rule; diving predators are cramped and easily found.

The Cistern

The great rainwater tank at the centre. Deep, dark, and full of nymphs that eat anything that floats.

Hunger is upkeep. Every caste keeps eating a share of what it cost, every day. A Matriarch who hatches a huge army in the first week starves her nurseries by the third — and starving nests lose Harmony, the pheromone bond that makes every nest work.

Nests

Home nests

Full yield. The Heartnest's scent strengthens Harmony nearby.

Taken nests

A quarter yield. Harmony near nothing. These go feral.

Bonded nests

Taken nests re-scented with a Bonding Chamber (costs Brood). Half yield; can hatch castes.

Forage-ground

Soil, stems, beds. Low yield unless you farm it. Ground never goes feral.

Harmony and the feral bloom

Every nest and ground has Harmony — how strongly it carries its Matriarch's scent. It drifts each dawn toward a target set by: distance from the Heartnest, how many wars the colony fights, enemies nearby, hunger, losses among the brood, and enemy scent-thieves. Chambers and calm neighbours raise it.

A taken nest below about a third Harmony may go feral — forget whose it is and turn on its keepers. Newly taken nests sit at a quarter. Once a day, at no fixed time, the scent fails or holds. If no soldier of yours is in the nest, it simply goes feral: its brood joins the Unscented, masterless swarms that wander and raid. A single garrison of your workers usually puts the bloom down.

Rules of battle

How colonies fight.

Clashes

When columns meet, the first clash is immediate; another every quarter-hour. Each strike counts against the target's hit class: a stag-pincer's jaws crush carapace and barely nick a soft worker.

The Heartchamber

Every nest and ground has a Heartchamber. A column settled in it is dug in: it takes far less harm and does not strike back. A column outside it, not in it, trades blows every clash.

Columns, flights, rafts

A column fights at full fury up to 16 castes, a flight up to 7, a raft-fleet up to 4. Larger than that, newcomers add less, then nothing, then confusion. A column moves at its slowest caste's pace.

Head, body, tail

Blows land mostly on a column's head, less on its body, little on its tail. A caste's place is set by its nature.

Spitting range

Bombardier beetles, puffball cannons and water bugs strike from afar. A target that cannot reach back cannot strike back. Spit, withdraw, wait out the quarter-hour, spit again.

Scent-marking

A nest or ground is yours only when a scent-marking caste reaches its Heartchamber. Beetles, flyers, rafts, mantids and paid symbionts cannot mark.

Canopy and understory

Dragonflies and bumblebees fly in the canopy, where spore-sentinels sense their wingbeat. Robber-flies and jewel-wasps hunt in the understory, below where any sentinel can feel them. Only archer-spitters, rafts and dragonflies catch understory flyers.

Within the column vs beside it

Spitters within the attacked column spit at every attacker and every spore, without tiring. Spitters merely beside it must refill their glands between volleys — send enough at once and some get through.

Scent, antennae, and stillness

Nearby castes are smelled; distant columns are a vague "foreign scent" until a scout identifies them. Antenna moths feel wingbeats and footfalls at range. Still castes — mantids, glasswings, nymphs — cannot be found until they strike, or until a hunter of their own kind comes close.

Shallows and Cistern

Striders love the lily shallows and drown in the Cistern. Nymphs are the reverse — cramped and visible among the pads, deadly in the deep tank.

Rafts

Columns cross water on woven rafts boarded at a raft dock. Rafts cannot fight and come apart easily; boarding and landing leave a column helpless for hours. Raft-Swarmers and Diving Beetles cross on their own.

The roost

Flyers sortie from a home roost and return to feed. Resting flyers are prey. Collapse the roost and its flight is grounded until it is rebuilt.

Hit classes

The three Ways

Each colony evolved along one Way. Same castes, different bodies.

The Carapace Way

Cheap, early, ground-bound. Titan beetles and puffball cannons in thrall from the first day, cheaply hatched. Bodies are thin-shelled and flyers are few, so Carapace colonies trust spore-sentinels to guard the air — and sentinels cannot feel the understory.

Field journal: the summer of the Ember War

Kept by Dr Wren Halloway, entomologist, who climbed in through a broken vent in May and did not leave until October.

3 JuneTwo ways to begin

The fire ants of the east beds — the colony I have started calling the Ember Dominion, after its colour and its manners — spent the first warm morning extending their larder galleries, and the afternoon on something I have never seen ants do. They herded beetles. Three enormous titan beetles, each a hundred times an ant's size, walked out of the Ember moult-yard by evening, crawling with handler-workers. By the fifth they were moving down the main paving toward the clover beds.

The moths of the north wall, on the other hand, did nothing visible at all. They spun new larder galleries in the ivy, and a large, elaborate roost, and a chamber I can only describe as a nursery for something that hadn't hatched yet. I sat by it for two days. Nothing came out.

In the ponds, the lily colonies wove two new rafts. One carried whirligig beetles. The other was crowded edge to edge with tiny archer-ants — the spitting kind — and nothing else. They floated both in the shallows by their own nest and left them there.

8–12 JuneThe paving

The clover hives sit on the best nectar in the glasshouse and have never had to fight for it. Their workers met the titans on the open flagstones and were ground into the cracks. In five days the Ember ants scent-marked four clover nests. I watched each one: a column of fire ants pours into the heartchamber, the air goes sharp with formic acid, and then — and this surprised me — the column leaves. All of it. Every soldier marches on toward the next nest. The marked nests are left full of bewildered bees and Ember scent and not one ant.

She's not garrisoning them. Why?

13 JuneOut of the ferns

The moth nursery opened at dawn. What came out were not moths but jewel-wasps — the metallic green parasitoids that sting beetles into paralysis — riding the moths' scent like an allegiance. Seven of them. They did not fly up into the open air under the glass. They flew down, into the fern jungle, and followed the drainage gully south along the ground.

The Ember column — three titans, two bombardier beetles, nothing else — was resting on the paving by the clover beds. Two tall fungal spore-sentinels the ants had cultivated stood a little way off, their caps tilted up, sensing the canopy for the wingbeat of dragonflies or bees. The canopy was empty. The wasps were in the understory, a centimetre off the stones, and the sentinels never felt them.

A titan beetle cannot reach its own back. The wasps landed on them one by one and stung. Every quarter-hour the wasps rose, circled under the fern-fronds and dropped again. By noon the titans were on their backs with their legs moving slowly, and the sentinels were still pointing serenely at an empty sky.

The sentinels had no idea. None.

15–18 JuneMore mushrooms

The Ember Matriarch's response was to cultivate more sentinels — eight more, ringing the paving — and to begin growing a vast mycelial dome over her own Heartnest, the most expensive thing I have seen any colony build. None of it can feel the understory. The wasps came back on the sixteenth, and the seventeenth. A few of the spitting archer-ants in each column would have shot them out of the ferns on the first pass; I have watched archer-ants knock down hoverflies. The Ember ants do not seem to value them. They rarely hatch any.

On the eighteenth a raft of lily-ants crossed to the moths' wall, stayed an hour, and crossed back. Since then the lily colonies and the moths have shared forage trails. An alliance, near as I can tell.

21 JuneThe raft

The Ember ants have a few flyers — seven hornets, which I believe they took from a wasp nest earlier in the spring. On the night of the twenty-first they sent all seven at the lily colonies' rafts, moored in the shallows: the whirligig raft, a raft carrying a giant water bug (the most dangerous thing in the pond, and quite unable to defend itself from above), and the raft of archer-ants, which I had stopped noticing weeks ago.

The archer raft was lashed in among the others. When the hornets dived on the water bug, every archer-ant on that raft spat at every hornet, all at once, again and again without stopping. I counted. Two hornets reached the water bug. One got back to the Ember roost. In the morning there were five small dark shapes floating among the lily pads.

Seven hornets. Lost to ants that can't even swim.

25–27 JuneFeral

Two of the four clover nests went feral on the same night. They had been sitting with a quarter of their old Harmony since the ants marked them, far from the Ember Heartnest, half-starved, and — of course — empty of Ember soldiers. When the scent failed there was nothing to stop it. By morning the bees had pulled down the formic markers and the nests had joined the Unscented. By the twenty-seventh an unscented swarm was raiding the Ember scent trail to the front.

2–5 JulyThe great column

The Ember Matriarch has gathered every caste she has into one column — I estimated thirty-one castes, nearly twice what a column can sensibly hold — and sent it against the moths' ivy fortress. I have never seen anything so large move so slowly. Half of it seemed to be trampling the other half.

The moths have put stag-pincer beetles and workers into the ivy nest's heartchamber, where they settled and simply endured. Two beds back, the moths' allies placed three puffball spore-cannons. Every quarter-hour the puffballs were rolled forward by their workers, burst a cloud of choking spores over the column from far beyond its reach, and were rolled back before it could close. The column never touched one. By the time it reached the ivy it was sick and slow, and it threw itself at defenders who barely bled.

9 JulyThe Cistern

For a second front the Ember Matriarch put two columns on rafts and sent them straight across the Cistern toward the lily colonies' far shore. Her own whirligig rafts were not yet woven; the troop rafts went alone. I knew what would happen and I could not look away. Two dragonfly nymphs had been sitting on the bottom of the tank, motionless, for nine days. Rafts have no defenders that can fight in the water. The nymphs came up from below, and the rafts came apart, and then the surface was still.

14–20 JulyThe roosts

The end, when it came, was a single night of homing spores — fungal bombs the moths' allies had been growing — that settled on both Ember roosts. Every flyer the ants had raised since June was resting there. Without flyers, and with sentinels that still could not feel the understory, the remaining Ember columns were hunted along the paving by jewel-wasps until there were none.

When the October cold came through the broken panes, the moths and the lily colonies held more than half the glasshouse between them. The Ember Heartnest went quiet in November.

What I think went wrong for the Ember ants

  1. Titans without archers. A titan cannot reach its own back. A few archer-ants in every column was the whole answer.
  2. Canopy sentinels against understory hunters. She grew a forest of the wrong mushroom.
  3. Hornets into the archer raft. Spitters in a raft-fleet spit at every attacker, every clash. The least impressive raft was the deadliest.
  4. Empty nests. Taken at a quarter Harmony and left without a single soldier, they went feral behind her.
  5. The thirty-one-caste column. Too large to fight, too slow to catch the puffballs.
  6. Attacking a settled heartchamber. Defenders who don't strike back also don't bleed.
  7. Troop rafts across the Cistern, unescorted. That is what nymphs are for.
  8. Flyers left resting in reach of spores. A grounded flight is a meal.
