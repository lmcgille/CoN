World III · Sport

Grand Theatre

Nobody has fought a real war in forty years. But every autumn, three billion people watch eight players fight one inside a simulation, live, for thirty simulated days. The sport is called Theatre. Its stars are the most famous people alive, and nearly all of them learned it at the same academy on the same small island.

Castellan Academy

Why a sport needs a single academy, and why rivals from every federation on Earth live in the same dorms.

Theatre is played in the Grand Rig, a full-immersion simulator. Players lie in neural cradles and feel the campaign as much as see it: the weight of the economy, the noise of a front, the shock when a fleet disappears. It is the most demanding sport ever invented, physically as well as mentally.

For its first decade anyone could build a rig, and people did. Then came the Ansan Incident of 2039: a sixteen-year-old played a bootleg forty-hour campaign on an unlicensed rig and never fully woke up. The World Theatre League responded with the Cradle Rules. Only accredited academies may put minors in a full-immersion rig, no player may turn professional without completing an accredited programme, and accreditation requires a licensed Grand Rig, a medical wing and the League's own instructors.

Building and running a licensed Grand Rig costs more than most national stadiums. One academy has ever managed it.

Why everyone ends up on the same island

Only one academy is accredited

Castellan Academy, on the Atlantic island of Ilha do Castelo, holds the only full accreditation. A second academy has been "two years away" for nine years. If you want to go pro, you go to Castellan.

Admission is blind

Every year forty national federations run open combines in sim-cafés and schools. The top scorers are invited regardless of country, money or sponsor. A kid from a Lagos sim-café and the heir to a rig manufacturer can arrive on the same ferry.

Federations want medals

The Federation Cup, Theatre's world cup, is played between national teams of Castellan graduates. Federations that hate each other on the pitch, at the ballot box or at the border still pay for their prodigies to train in the same building as their rivals.

Houses, not nations

League rules forbid national dorms or national teams inside the academy. Cadets are drafted into four Houses, Anvil, Lantern, Current and Thorn, in a televised draft, and the House Cup is the most-watched amateur event in the sport.

The rivalries inside the walls

Federations

Old sporting grudges travel with the cadets. Some federations have not beaten each other in a Federation Cup in a generation, and their cadets know it.

Sponsors

The two great sponsors, Halvorsen Rigs and Kestrel Energy, scout cadets from first year. A sponsored cadet who loses to a rival's cadet costs somebody money.

Money

Rig-kids grew up with home simulators. Café-kids learned on public machines with time limits. They play very differently and resent each other accordingly.

Handles, kits and the Mask Rule

Two years after the Ansan Incident came the Kowal case. A fifteen-year-old prodigy's face, school and home address were posted by fans after a viral match, and her family had to move twice. The League added the Mask Rule to the Cradle Rules. Until a cadet graduates and turns pro, nobody may know or publish their real name, face, nationality or age: not fans, not sponsors, not even other cadets. Federations know which cadets they sent, and no one else does. The rule also keeps admissions and grading blind, and stops sponsors and federations pressuring children they cannot identify.

Handles

Every cadet chooses a handle at intake. By custom, cadets call each other "they": the suit hides everything else, and nobody wants to guess. It has to be approved by the League: no real names, no national references, nothing already registered. The House Cup is broadcast to millions using handles only. Most pros keep their academy handle for life.

The rig suit

The Grand Rig needs a full-body haptic suit and a helmet with a neural cowl. Sessions are called on short notice, and suiting up takes forty minutes, so cadets wear their rig suits all day. Under the Mask Rule, visors stay dark everywhere outside the private sleep pods, and helmet speakers modulate voices.

Why suits can be customised

Theatre is a sport, and sport runs on fans. Fans pick favourites by what they can see, and replica kits are the League's second-biggest income. So every cadet designs a kit, the suit's livery, within League rules: no flags, no names, no national colours. Sponsors who scout a cadet may pay for cosmetic upgrades. Kit sales fund the café-kid scholarships.

Reading a player at a glance

Every rig suit is the same League-standard shell, so the identity is in five places. The shoulder colour gives the House. The handle and number sit across the back. Each helmet carries a registered crest silhouette, like a racing helmet design. Each visor has a personal glow colour, and the kit pattern covers the rest. A fan in the stands can name a cadet from the crest alone.

The sport

How Theatre is played, broadcast and won.

A Theatre campaign is a thirty-day war on a fictional continent generated fresh for each season. The Rig runs at high speed: one sim-day takes about an hour, and the Rig pauses for the players' "nights," so a full campaign plays out over a broadcast week. Each player commands a nation, its economy, its army and its diplomacy, all at once and alone.

Fans watch through the Theatre Feed: a god's-eye map, live player cameras inside the cradles, two commentators, and a ticker showing every nation's Standing, the in-sim score. Standing comes from cities, provinces and three Crown Sites on each season's map. The first player or alliance to reach the Standing target at a daily count wins.

Formats

Melee

Eight players, free-for-all. Alliances are legal, are formed live on air, and can be broken at any time. Betrayal is not against the rules. Fans love it, and sponsors pretend not to.

Pairs

Four teams of two. Each pair's Standing is pooled, and so is its target. A pair whose partner falls asleep at the wheel is in trouble.

Federation Cup

The world cup: national teams of three in a twelve-player campaign. Held every four years and watched by everyone.

The season map: Halvorsund

This season's generated continent is Halvorsund: salt plains in the centre, a broken mountain spine in the north, a fjord coast in the south with shallow sounds and a deep cold sea beyond, and a strip of storm forest down the east. Its three Crown Sites are the Lighthouse, the Old Observatory and the Deepwater Gate.

Territory

Home cities

Full output. The capital boosts Spirit nearby.

Captured cities

A quarter output, and Spirit near the floor. These are the cities that rebel.

Integrated cities

Captured cities brought in with an Integration Office (costs Recruits). Half output; can raise units.

Provinces

Countryside. Low output unless you build a Works. Provinces never rebel.

Spirit and the Free Cities

Every city and province has Spirit from 0 to 100. Each sim-dawn it drifts toward a target set by distance from your capital, the number of wars you are in, enemy units nearby, shortages, collateral damage, enemy spies, and the buildings you have placed.

A captured city below about a third Spirit may rebel, and a freshly captured city starts at a quarter. Once per sim-day, at a random time, it rolls. If no unit of yours is inside, it simply joins the Free Cities, an AI faction that raises militia and wanders. Even one garrison unit usually wins the fight. The Feed plays a siren sound for every rebellion. Fans call it "the doorbell."

Rules of play

The Theatre ruleset, as it is printed in every rookie's handbook.

Rounds

The first round resolves the instant two forces touch, and another follows every sim-hour. Each round applies the attacker's rating against the defender's hit class.

The flag-point

Every city and province has a flag-point. Units sitting on it are dug in: they take much less damage and do not hit back. Units near it but not on it trade blows every round.

Squads, flights, fleets

A ground squad fights at full strength up to 11 units, like a football team, which is where the name comes from. An air flight fights at full strength up to 4 and a fleet up to 6. Beyond that, each extra unit adds less, then nothing, then actively hurts. A group moves at its slowest unit's speed.

Front, middle, back

Incoming damage lands mostly on the front, less on the middle, least on the back. The unit type decides where it stands.

Range

Mortars, Volley batteries and Dreadnoughts fire from range, and a target without similar reach cannot answer. Fire, pull back, wait out the round, fire again. Commentators call it "the yo-yo."

Only infantry plants the flag

A city or province changes hands only when a flag-planting infantry unit reaches its flag-point. Vehicles, aircraft, ships, Ghosts and Hired Guns cannot plant flags.

High and low air

Jets fly high, where Umbrella batteries track them. Rotor units fly low, underneath Umbrella and Dome coverage entirely. Only Swatters, Halo frigates and Falcons can touch low air.

Inside the group vs nearby

Air defence inside the targeted group fires at every attacker and every missile, with no cooldown. Air defence merely covering a nearby group has a cooldown, so a big enough salvo saturates it.

Fog, scouting and stealth

You see what is near your units. Distant groups show as grey markers until scouted. Beacons and Eye aircraft see further. Stealth units are invisible until they fire or a matching detector comes close.

Shallows and deep water

Pickets love the fjord sounds and suffer in the deep sea. Submarines are the reverse.

Transports

Infantry crossing water boards transports at a harbour. Transports cannot fight, and boarding or landing leaves units helpless for hours. Mariners and Otters land on any beach.

Airfields

Aircraft fly from a home airfield and return to rearm. Parked aircraft are helpless. Destroy the airfield and everything on it is grounded.

Chronicle II: The House Cup

Third year at Castellan. The Pairs tournament between Houses, broadcast under handles only, from the private journal of the cadet called Moss.

Draw nightPairs

The draw put Northlight with a second-year called Pebble for House Lantern, and Riptide with Gambit for House Current. I got nobody. Odd-numbered year, so one cadet plays alone as a "free nation." That's me. Twenty, for Thorn, is out with a cradle headache, and their crest has been drooping at everyone all week. Pairs pool their Standing, and their target is doubled. Everybody knows it, and not everybody thinks about it.

Sim-day 6Gambit's guests

Gambit and Northlight signed a passage deal on day one so Gambit's convoys could cross Lantern land. Their Pikes escorted the convoys and sat on the flag-point at Fennmouth between runs. On day six Gambit declared war on Northlight. They didn't move a single unit. Their Pikes were already standing on Fennmouth's flag-point, and the city changed colour in front of the whole common room. Northlight actually stood up out of their cradle, visor flashing gold.

Sim-day 7–12Pebble goes quiet

Pebble froze. They built factories, sat in them, and stopped answering Northlight's messages. They're still in Northlight's pair, so Northlight still needs double Standing, and Pebble is contributing nothing. Northlight is basically carrying a sandbag up a hill. Coach Kell said nothing during the session, which is worse than if he'd said something.

Sim-day 10–14Two directions

Northlight tried the yo-yo, of course. They've watched the Grand Final replay a hundred times. Volley batteries in, fire, out. Riptide was ready. They sent their main squad at Northlight as bait, and while Northlight's batteries were pulling back they closed two small groups of Rangers and Scouts from the north and the south at the same time. A battery can run from one thing, but it can't run from two. Gone in a round.

Sim-day 15–17The spy and the salvo

Riptide put a spy in Northlight's capital on day two. On day fifteen the spy saw that Northlight's main airfield was under a Dome, but they'd moved its Swatters forward. On day seventeen Riptide's Sentinels fired twelve Darts in one salvo. The Dome got one, cooled down, got another. Most of the rest landed, and half their air force died on the runway. They've been using the Dome as a security blanket. Apparently domes have cooldowns. Everyone in the common room learned that at the same time.

Sim-day 18–22Shades

Northlight answered with Shades, the stealth bombers. Riptide's Dome hadn't researched stealth detection, and for five sim-days their harbours burned out of an empty sky. They got the upgrade on day twenty-two. On day twenty-three, they saw them.

Sim-day 20–24Mines

Northlight also sent two Sentinels into Gambit's harbour sound at night and laid mines. Gambit's pickets reported "faint sonar contacts, possibly debris." Three pickets and two transports later, Gambit closed their own harbour.

Sim-day 25The Nova

Northlight had Fennmouth surrounded and Gambit was going to lose it. So Gambit used the Nova. It's legal. Everyone thinks it's disgusting. It broke the siege and it also wiped out Gambit's own garrison inside the city, and the Rig marked Fennmouth as dead ground. Every one of Gambit's cities lost Spirit at once. Three rebelled on day twenty-eight. The whole common room booed, including people from their own House. Their sponsor posted something about "bold choices" and then deleted it.

Sim-day 29–30Riptide leaves, and I collect

With Gambit collapsing, Riptide's pair still needed double. On day twenty-nine they left the pair. They didn't tell Gambit; they just did it. At the next count Riptide alone cleared the solo target, and House Current won the Cup.

The final count wasn't until midnight sim-time, though. I had fought nobody for thirty days. So I spent the last day walking Pikes and Droppers into every empty province on Gambit's collapsing border. Eleven. I finished third overall. Coach Kell put my replay on the screen at breakfast the next morning and said nothing at all, which from him is applause.

Coach Kell's notes on the House Cup

  1. A passage deal is a loaded gun. Units on your flag-point can take it the moment their owner declares.
  2. A frozen partner costs you. Every member raises the pooled target.
  3. The yo-yo has a counter. Two directions, same round.
  4. Spies compound. Placed on day two, decisive on day seventeen.
  5. A Dome can be saturated. It has a cooldown, but Swatters in the target do not.
  6. An empty sky can still burn your harbour. Research detection before you need it.
  7. Mines look like debris. Treat any unexplained contact in a harbour as a minefield.
  8. The Nova costs more than it wins. It wins one siege and crashes your whole nation's Spirit, and your reputation with it.
