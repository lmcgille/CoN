On the Smoking Coast the great beasts never died out, and the people learned to ride them. When the fire-mountain woke and the old hunting grounds burned, six clans came down to the Grass-Lake country with their herds. There was not room for all of them.

The world

A warm, volcanic coast where people ride the largest animals that ever lived.

The Smoking Coast curves around a warm sea under a ring of volcanoes. Its plains are grass; its valleys are fern-forest; its cliffs are full of rookeries where the great winged beasts nest. In the sea live things longer than any boat.

The clans of the coast are herders and riders. Each clan holds a great camp — its capital — some villages and the range between them. They raise beasts from eggs, bind them with songs and salt, and ride them to war. When the fire-mountain Ash-Mother woke last spring and buried the northern ranges, the clans came south, and the war began.

Victory is Standing: at each full moon, the elders of all clans meet at the Speaking Stone and count which clan or clan-bond holds the most villages, range and the three Sacred Places — the Hot Springs, the Bone Field and the Speaking Stone itself.

Country

The Grass Plains

Open grass to the horizon. The big horned beasts charge at full strength; warriors on foot are trampled.

Villages

Stockades, longhouses and narrow lanes. Big beasts cannot turn; warriors dug in behind the stakes are hard to shift.

The Fire Hills

Broken lava country. Cliff-Climbers move easily; beasts break legs.

The Fern Valleys

Dense, wet, dark. Slow for all, murderous for the great beasts.

The Reef Lagoons

Warm shallow water inside the reef. Canoes are quick; the great sea-hunters are cramped and easily seen.

The Blue Deep

Outside the reef. Mosasaurs rule; small boats are swamped.

A herd is a mouth. Every beast and warrior eats a share of what it cost, every day. A chieftain who breaks a great herd in the first moon is short of forage by the third — and hungry villages lose Kinship, which makes every village gather less.

Villages and range

Home villages

Full gathering. The great camp strengthens Kinship nearby.

Taken villages

A quarter gathering; Kinship near nothing. These break the bond.

Adopted villages

Taken villages brought in by an Adoption feast (costs Kin). Half gathering; can raise warriors.

Range

Open country. Little gathered unless you set up a camp on it. Range never rebels.

Kinship and the Lost

Every village and range has Kinship — how strongly its people feel they belong to you. It drifts each dawn toward what they feel: how far your great camp is, how many feuds you are in, enemies on the range, hunger, burning, and whisperers sent by other clans. Feasting halls and peaceful neighbours raise it.

A taken village below a third Kinship may break the bond. Fresh conquests sit at a quarter. Once a day, at a time no one can say, the bond holds or snaps. If none of your warriors stand by its fire-circle, the village simply joins the Lost, clanless bands who answer to nobody and raid where they like. One band of your warriors at the fire usually ends it.

Ways of war

Clashes

The first clash comes when war-herds meet; another every half-hand of the sun (half an hour). Each blow uses its strength against the target's hit class. A bone-breaker's ram shatters a horned beast and barely touches a warrior.

The fire-circle

Every village and range has a fire-circle. A war-herd standing in it is dug in: it takes far less harm and does not strike back. A herd beside it, not in it, trades blows every clash.

Herds, flocks, fleets

A war-herd fights at full fury up to 15, a flock up to 6, a fleet up to 4. More than that and newcomers add less, then nothing, then trample their own side. A herd moves at its slowest member's pace.

Horn, flank, tail

Blows land mostly on a herd's horn, less on its flank, least on its tail. What a beast or warrior is decides where it goes.

Throwing range

Stone-throwers, spitter-beasts and turtle-ships strike from afar. A target that can't reach back can't strike back. Throw, fall back, wait out the half-hand, throw again.

Raising the totem

A village or range is yours only when totem-raising warriors stand in its fire-circle. Beasts, flyers, boats, stalkers and outcasts can win the fight but raise nothing.

High sky and low sky

Sky-lords and fire-wings soar high, where harpoon ballistae can track them. Dart-wings and hook-beaks fly low, weaving through the treetops, too fast and too low for a ballista crew to follow. Only slingers, sling-rafts and sky-lords can bring them down.

In the herd or beside it

Slingers in the attacked herd sling at every flyer and every fire-spear, without tiring. Slingers merely beside it must gather stones between volleys — send enough at once and some get through.

Seeing and hiding

Nearby herds are seen; distant ones are only dust until a scout names them. Drum-hill watchers and cloud-seers see far. Hidden things — stalkers, night-gliders, mosasaurs — cannot be seen until they strike or something that hunts their kind comes near.

Lagoon and Deep

Canoes love the lagoons and drown in the Deep. Mosasaurs are the reverse.

War-canoes

Warriors crossing water ride war-canoes boarded at a canoe beach. Canoes cannot fight; boarding and landing leave warriors helpless for a while. Canoe-Raiders and swamp-wallowers cross on their own.

The rookery

Flyers go out from a home rookery and return to rest. A resting flyer is prey. Burn the rookery and its flock is stuck on the ground.

Hit classes

The three bloods

Every clan is born to one of the three bloods. Same kinds of beasts and warriors; different breeds and different seasons.

Earth-blood

Cheap, early, ground-bound. Horned titans and fire-pot slingers in the first moon. Smaller, thinner-hided breeds and few flyers, so Earth-blood clans trust harpoon ballistae against the sky — and ballistae cannot follow a low flyer.

War-herds

The telling: How the Redclaws Lost the Grass

As the Reef Folk tell it to their children, with the Cloud-Cliff verses kept in.

Sit close. Feed the fire. This is the telling of the Grass War.

The first moonTwo ways to start a war

When the Ash-Mother woke, Kesra Bloodfern of the Redclaws did not mourn. She built longhouses, because a chief who does not build longhouses is soon a chief of nothing. And then she went to the breaking pens. By the end of the first moon she had three horned titans broken to the saddle — beasts as tall as four longhouses, with horns like felled trees — and behind them her spear-clans and her spitter-beasts, and she pointed all of it at the Grass-Lake country.

Anwe of the Cloud-Cliff People broke no beasts at all. She built longhouses and a story lodge, and she made the rookery cliffs bigger, and her young warriors went to her and said: Speaker, the Redclaws have titans. And Anwe said: then the rookery is not big enough yet.

Down in the lagoons, Tide-Mother Ollo lashed together two new rafts. One carried a plesiosaur-drawn hunter boat. The other was only a wide raft of logs, crowded edge to edge with slingers, every one of them with a bag of round stones. She anchored both inside her own reef.

The second moonThe Grass

The Grass Plains are the titans' country. Old Hunter Tamsa's people stood in the open grass, because that is where their herds were, and the titans went through them like wind through barley. In five days Kesra raised her totem in four Grass-Lake villages. And each time, the titans went on. Each time, the spear-clans followed the titans. No warrior stayed by any of the four fires.

The ninth dayOut of the trees

Then Anwe's rookery opened. Out flew six hook-beaks — small winged beasts with claws that can tear through hide a hand thick. They did not climb into the high sky, where the Redclaw harpoon ballistae were waiting. They flew down, into the Fern Valleys, and along the river under the treetops.

Kesra's lead war-herd — three titans and two spitter-beasts, and no one else — was drinking at the river ford. Two great harpoon ballistae stood on a rise behind them, their crews watching the sky. They watched it all afternoon. The sky was empty. The hook-beaks came out of the ferns at the height of a man's head, and the crews never got a harpoon near them.

A titan cannot look up. Its neck frill is too heavy. The hook-beaks landed on the titans' backs and tore. Every half-hand they rose into the trees and came again. By sundown there were three great shapes lying in the river, and the ballista crews were still swearing the sky had been empty.

The sky was empty, they said. The sky was empty.

The third moonMore harpoons

Kesra's answer was more ballistae — eight more — and wind-callers on the hill of her great camp, the most costly thing an Earth-blood clan can raise. None of them could see a low flyer. The hook-beaks came again, and again. One band of slingers walking with each war-herd would have filled the air with stones and knocked the hook-beaks out of it. Earth-blood warriors think slings are for children minding goats.

That moon Anwe and Ollo cut their palms and pressed them together at the Speaking Stone. Their Standing would be counted as one.

The fourth moonThe sling-raft

Kesra had a few flyers: six fire-wings, bought from the Reef Folk long before the war, that drop burning pots of pitch. Her scouts found Ollo's fleet anchored in the lagoon: the plesiosaur hunter-boat, a great turtle-ship — a sea-turtle bigger than a longhouse with a war-platform on its back, the most terrible thing in the lagoon and helpless against anything from above — and the raft of logs, which the scouts did not even count as a boat.

The raft was tied in among them. When the fire-wings came down on the turtle-ship, every slinger on that raft let fly at every fire-wing, and again, and again, without ever stopping to gather stones. The air over the lagoon was full of stones. Two fire-wings reached the turtle. One flew home.

Six fire-wings went out. One came home. They were killed by a raft of logs.

The fifth moonThe bond breaks

The four Grass-Lake villages had been sullen since the Redclaws took them, far from Kesra's great camp, hungry, and with no Redclaw warrior by any fire. On one night, two of them broke the bond. There was no one to stop it. By morning their people had joined the Lost, and soon a Lost war-band was raiding the trail that fed Kesra's front.

The sixth moonThe siege of Cloud-Foot

Kesra gathered everything she had left — twenty-eight beasts and bands, nearly twice what any war-chief can hold together — and drove it at Cloud-Foot, the Cloud-Cliff village under the rookeries. It moved like a landslide. It fought like a herd in a panic, half of it trampling the other half.

Anwe put bone-breakers and spear-clans in Cloud-Foot's fire-circle, where they stood and endured. Two ranges back, she set three fire-pot slinger crews. Every half-hand they ran forward, hurled burning pots into the great herd from beyond its reach, and ran back before it could close. Not one of them was caught. The herd reached Cloud-Foot burned and slow, and broke itself on warriors who barely bled.

The seventh moonThe Blue Deep

For a second front Kesra put two war-bands into war-canoes and sent them straight across the Blue Deep to come at the Reef Folk from the south. Her own hunter-boats were not finished. The canoes went alone. Two of Ollo's mosasaurs had been lying deep for nine days, waiting for exactly that. Canoes do not fight. The Blue Deep was very calm afterwards.

The eighth moonThe rookeries burn

The last of it was fire-spears — spears that a bound spirit-bird carries to the place you name — falling on both Redclaw rookeries in one night. Every flyer Kesra had raised since the fourth moon was resting there. With no flyers, and ballistae that still could not see a hook-beak, the last Redclaw herds were hunted across the Grass. At the full moon the elders counted, and the Cloud-Cliff and the Reef together stood above the Redclaws, and the Grass War was done.

And that is why your mother makes you carry a sling.

What the elders said

  1. Titans without slingers. A titan cannot look up. One band of slingers per herd was all it needed.
  2. Harpoons against low flyers. She raised the most costly defence there is, aimed at the wrong sky.
  3. Fire-wings against the sling-raft. Slingers in a fleet sling at every attacker, every clash. The least of the boats was the deadliest.
  4. Empty fire-circles. Taken villages at a quarter Kinship, with no warriors, broke the bond behind her.
  5. The twenty-eight herd. Too big to fight well, too slow to catch the fire-pots.
  6. Charging a held fire-circle. Warriors who do not strike back barely bleed.
  7. Canoes across the Deep, alone. That is what mosasaurs wait for.
  8. Flyers resting where fire-spears can reach. A grounded flock is meat.
